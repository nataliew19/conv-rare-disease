## Hemophilia A

Hemophilia A, also known as classical hemophilia [1], is a hereditary bleeding disorder characterized by the blood's inability to clot properly, leading to prolonged bleeding after injury, surgery, or even spontaneously [2][1][3]. This condition stems from a deficiency or complete absence of clotting factor VIII (FVIII) [2][4][1], a crucial protein in the coagulation cascade that facilitates blood clot formation [2]. As the most prevalent type of hemophilia [2] and the most common X-linked hereditary disorder affecting hemostasis [1], Hemophilia A presents a significant health challenge.

Approximately 1 in 5,000 newborn males are affected by Hemophilia A [3], with a substantial proportion, around 60%, experiencing the severe form of the disease [3]. The severity of hemophilia A is determined by the level of functioning FVIII in the blood. Treatment options range from on-demand or prophylactic replacement therapy with FVIII concentrates to newer therapies such as monoclonal antibodies and gene therapy, which are revolutionizing the management of this condition [8][9].

## Overview / Definition

Hemophilia A is a genetic bleeding disorder in which the blood does not clot normally due to a deficiency or absence of clotting factor VIII (FVIII) [1][2][4]. FVIII is an essential protein that participates in the coagulation cascade, a series of enzymatic reactions that ultimately lead to the formation of a stable blood clot. When FVIII is deficient or absent, the coagulation cascade is disrupted, resulting in prolonged bleeding [2]. Without adequate FVIII, even minor injuries can result in significant blood loss, and spontaneous bleeding episodes can occur [5].

## Signs & Symptoms

The hallmark symptom of Hemophilia A is prolonged bleeding [1][3], which can manifest in various ways. Bleeding can occur internally, into joints and muscles, or externally, from minor cuts or injuries [5][6]. The severity of bleeding is directly related to the amount of functional FVIII present in the blood [5][6]. Normal FVIII levels typically range from 50% to 150% [5].

The clinical presentation of Hemophilia A varies depending on the severity of the FVIII deficiency [3][5]:

*   **Mild Hemophilia A:** Individuals with 6% to 49% of normal FVIII levels generally experience bleeding only after significant injury, trauma, or surgical procedures [5][3].
*   **Moderate Hemophilia A:** Individuals with 1% to 5% of normal FVIII levels tend to have bleeding episodes after injuries [5][3].
*   **Severe Hemophilia A:** Individuals with less than 1% of normal FVIII levels may experience frequent spontaneous bleeding episodes, often into joints and muscles [5][3].

Other common signs and symptoms of Hemophilia A include excessive bruising [2], frequent nosebleeds [2][6], blood in the urine or stool [6], and bleeding into the joints, known as hemarthrosis [2][6]. Internal bleeding, particularly in the brain, represents a serious and potentially life-threatening complication [6].

## Genetics & Pathophysiology

Hemophilia A is primarily caused by an inherited mutation in the F8 gene located on the X chromosome [4][3]. This X-linked inheritance pattern explains the higher prevalence of Hemophilia A in males [2][4][3]. Females, possessing two X chromosomes, can often compensate for a mutated F8 gene on one chromosome with a normal copy on the other [4]. In contrast, males, having only one X chromosome, will develop Hemophilia A if their single X chromosome carries the mutated F8 gene [4].

A woman carrying a mutated F8 gene is considered a carrier and has a 50% chance of passing the mutated gene to her children [4]. If a male inherits the mutated gene, he will develop Hemophilia A. All female children of men with Hemophilia A will inherit the mutated gene and become carriers, while male children will not inherit the mutated gene from their fathers [4]. The mutations in the F8 gene disrupt the production of functional FVIII protein, leading to a deficiency in the blood clotting process [1].

## Diagnosis

The diagnosis of Hemophilia A involves a comprehensive evaluation of blood clotting function [5]. Initially, blood tests are performed to assess the overall clotting time. A prolonged clotting time may indicate a clotting disorder, prompting further investigation [7]. A crucial diagnostic step is the clotting factor assay, which specifically measures the level of FVIII in the blood [5]. This assay confirms the diagnosis of Hemophilia A and determines its severity based on the FVIII level [5]. Additional blood tests may include a complete blood count, screening tests of clotting times such as prothrombin time (PT) and activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT), and mixing studies [7].

## Management / Treatment

The primary treatment for Hemophilia A is replacement therapy [8], which involves intravenously injecting concentrates of clotting factor VIII to compensate for the deficiency [8]. FVIII concentrates can be derived from human plasma (plasma-derived FVIII) or manufactured using recombinant DNA technology (recombinant FVIII) [8].

Several treatment modalities are available for managing Hemophilia A [7][8][9]:

*   **Replacement Therapy:** Regular infusions of FVIII concentrates to prevent bleeding episodes (prophylaxis) or to treat acute bleeding events (on-demand therapy) [8].
*   **Monoclonal Antibody Therapy:** Newer medications, such as emicizumab (Hemlibra) and Marstacimab-hncq (Hympavzi), are monoclonal antibodies that mimic the function of FVIII and promote blood clotting by bridging factor IXa and factor X [9].
*   **Targeted Therapy:** Medications like fitusiran (Qfitla) reduce bleeding by interfering with the production of antithrombin, a natural anticoagulant [9].
*   **Gene Therapy:** Innovative gene therapy approaches, such as Roctavian, deliver a functional F8 gene to liver cells, enabling them to produce FVIII and potentially providing a long-term cure [9].
*   **DDAVP (desmopressin acetate):** For individuals with mild Hemophilia A, DDAVP can be used to stimulate the release of stored FVIII from the body's own cells [7][8].
*   **Amicar (epsilon amino caproic acid):** This medication helps prevent blood clots from breaking down and is often used to control bleeding in the mouth or after dental procedures [7].

## Prognosis / Natural History

The prognosis for individuals with Hemophilia A has significantly improved due to advances in treatment [5][9]. With regular prophylactic treatment and prompt management of bleeding episodes, people with Hemophilia A can lead relatively normal lives [5]. However, without adequate treatment, recurrent bleeding into joints can lead to chronic joint damage (hemarthropathy), causing pain, disability, and a reduced quality of life [6].

The development of inhibitors (antibodies against FVIII) is a serious complication that can render FVIII replacement therapy ineffective [6]. The development of newer therapies like monoclonal antibody therapy and gene therapy offer hope for improving the long-term outcomes for individuals with Hemophilia A [9].

## Epidemiology

Hemophilia A affects approximately 1 in 5,000 newborn males [3]. The condition occurs worldwide and affects all racial and ethnic groups equally [3]. About 60% of individuals with Hemophilia A have the severe form of the disease [3].

## History / Nomenclature

Hemophilia, including Hemophilia A, has been recognized for centuries. Historical accounts of bleeding disorders date back to ancient times [3]. Queen Victoria of England was a known carrier of hemophilia, and her descendants spread the gene to several royal families in Europe, leading to hemophilia being referred to as the "royal disease" [3]. The specific deficiency of factor VIII in Hemophilia A was identified in the mid-20th century, leading to the development of targeted replacement therapies [1].

## Research & Trials

Ongoing research efforts are focused on developing new and improved treatments for Hemophilia A [9]. Gene therapy holds great promise as a potential cure, with clinical trials showing encouraging results [9]. Researchers are also working on developing novel clotting factor concentrates with extended half-lives, reducing the frequency of infusions required for prophylaxis [9]. The development of new monoclonal antibody therapies and targeted therapies provides additional treatment options for individuals with Hemophilia A, particularly those who have developed inhibitors [9].

## Uncertainties & Controversies

While significant progress has been made in the treatment of Hemophilia A, several uncertainties and controversies remain. The optimal approach to prophylactic treatment, including the starting age and dosage of FVIII, is still debated [8]. The long-term effects of gene therapy are not yet fully understood, and questions remain regarding the durability of gene expression and the potential for late-onset complications [9]. The high cost of some of the newer therapies, such as gene therapy and monoclonal antibodies, raises concerns about access and affordability [9].
