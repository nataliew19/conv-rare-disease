## Rett Syndrome

**Lead / Summary**

Rett syndrome is a rare genetic neurological and developmental disorder that primarily affects females [1][2]. This condition disrupts brain development, leading to a progressive loss of motor skills and language [1]. While there is no cure for Rett syndrome, early diagnosis and comprehensive management strategies can significantly improve the quality of life for affected individuals [1][2].

The onset of Rett syndrome typically occurs between 6 and 18 months of age [2]. At this stage, children may start missing developmental milestones and losing previously acquired abilities [2]. Symptoms vary among individuals, but common signs include loss of skills, problems with muscle control, unusual hand movements, seizures, intellectual disabilities, and slowed growth [1][2]. Treatment focuses on alleviating symptoms through medications, therapies, and supportive care [1][2].

**Overview / Definition**

Rett syndrome is defined as a rare genetic neurological and developmental disorder that primarily affects females [1][2]. It is characterized by a period of normal early development followed by a regression in motor, communication, and cognitive skills [1]. This regression typically begins between 6 and 18 months of age [2]. The syndrome disrupts brain development, leading to a progressive loss of previously acquired abilities [1].

**Signs & Symptoms**

The signs and symptoms of Rett syndrome can vary significantly from one individual to another [2]. Some of the most common symptoms include the loss of previously acquired skills such as crawling, walking, communication, or hand use [1]. Individuals may also experience increasing problems with the muscles that control movement, coordination, and communication [1]. Unusual hand movements, such as repetitive rubbing or clapping, are also characteristic of the condition [1]. Other symptoms can include seizures, intellectual disabilities [1], slowed physical growth, including height, weight, and head size [2], autistic-like symptoms, such as social isolation or reduced eye contact [2], breathing problems, including breath-holding and hyperventilation [1], heart rhythm abnormalities [2], and difficulties with chewing and swallowing [1].

**Genetics & Pathophysiology**

Rett syndrome is primarily caused by mutations in the *MECP2* gene, which is located on the X chromosome [2]. This gene plays a crucial role in brain development and the regulation of other gene functions [2]. The majority of *MECP2* mutations occur spontaneously and are not inherited [2]. The mutations disrupt normal brain function, leading to the characteristic symptoms of Rett syndrome [2].

**Diagnosis**

The diagnosis of Rett syndrome involves a clinical evaluation, genetic testing, and careful observation of early developmental signs [2]. Genetic testing can identify mutations in the *MECP2* gene [1]. The diagnostic process often involves a multidisciplinary team of specialists, including neurologists, geneticists, and developmental pediatricians [2].

**Management / Treatment**

There is currently no cure for Rett syndrome [1]. Treatment focuses on managing symptoms and improving the overall quality of life for affected individuals [2]. A multidisciplinary approach is essential, involving a team of healthcare professionals [2]. Potential treatments include medications to control seizures, muscle stiffness, and problems with breathing, sleep, the GI tract, or the heart [1]. Physical therapy helps maintain movement, improve posture and balance, and enhance walking skills [1]. Occupational therapy focuses on improving hand use and teaching self-directed activities [2]. Speech therapy aims to maximize communication abilities [2]. Nutritional support is important to address difficulties with chewing and swallowing [2]. Braces or surgery may be necessary for scoliosis [2]. Regular monitoring for heart abnormalities is also crucial [2]. Special education programs are often beneficial [2]. In 2023, the FDA approved Trofinetide, a drug to treat Rett syndrome in children ages two and older [1].

**Prognosis / Natural History**

Rett syndrome is a lifelong condition [2]. The severity and progression of symptoms can vary considerably among affected individuals [2]. While the condition presents significant challenges, with appropriate management and support, individuals with Rett syndrome can achieve meaningful quality of life [1].

**Uncertainties & Controversies**

Further research is needed to fully elucidate the pathophysiology of the condition and identify potential therapeutic targets [1].
