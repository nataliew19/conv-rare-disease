## Cystic Fibrosis

**Lead / Summary**

Cystic fibrosis (CF) is an inherited genetic disorder characterized by the buildup of thick, sticky mucus that can damage multiple organs in the body [1][2]. The lungs and digestive system are most severely affected, leading to chronic respiratory infections and difficulties in nutrient absorption [3][2]. While there is currently no cure for CF, advancements in treatment have significantly improved the quality of life and life expectancy for individuals living with this condition [7][9]. Treatment focuses on managing symptoms, preventing complications, and supporting overall health [9][10].

CF is caused by mutations in a specific gene that regulates the movement of salt and water across cell membranes [4]. The severity of the condition can vary widely depending on the specific mutation(s) inherited [3]. Early diagnosis through newborn screening and sweat testing is crucial for initiating timely treatment and improving long-term outcomes [5][7]. Ongoing research continues to explore new therapies and potential cures for CF [7].

**Overview / Definition**

Cystic fibrosis (CF) is a genetic disorder that disrupts normal cell function, leading to the production of abnormally thick and sticky mucus [1][2]. This mucus can clog various organs, primarily the lungs, pancreas, intestines, liver, sweat glands, and reproductive organs [3][2]. The buildup of mucus in the lungs makes it difficult to breathe and creates a breeding ground for bacteria, leading to chronic infections [3]. In the digestive system, thick mucus can block the release of digestive enzymes from the pancreas, impairing nutrient absorption [6].

**Signs & Symptoms**

The signs and symptoms of cystic fibrosis can vary in severity and onset, and can differ from person to person [3]. Respiratory symptoms include persistent coughing with thick mucus or phlegm, wheezing, shortness of breath, chronic respiratory infections (such as pneumonia and bronchitis), and nasal polyps [3]. Digestive symptoms can include loose, greasy stools, poor weight gain and growth (failure to thrive), and intestinal blockages [6]. Other possible signs include clubbing of the fingers and toes, very salty-tasting skin, and meconium ileus (intestinal blockage) in newborns [1]. Because CF affects the sweat glands, individuals with CF have abnormally high levels of salt in their sweat [1].

**Genetics & Pathophysiology**

Cystic fibrosis is caused by mutations in the cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) gene [4]. This gene provides instructions for making a protein that regulates the movement of chloride ions and water across cell membranes [4]. These movements are essential for producing thin, freely flowing mucus, sweat, and digestive juices [4]. When the CFTR gene is mutated, the protein is defective, resulting in disrupted chloride transport and the production of thick, sticky mucus [4]. Individuals must inherit two copies of the mutated gene (one from each parent) to develop CF [4]. There are over 1,700 known mutations of the CF gene, with varying effects on CFTR protein function and disease severity [5].

**Diagnosis**

Most individuals with cystic fibrosis are diagnosed by the age of two, although diagnosis can occur later in life [5]. Newborn screening programs often detect CF shortly after birth [7]. If newborn screening is positive, or if a healthcare provider suspects CF based on symptoms, further testing is necessary [5]. The sweat test is the standard diagnostic test for CF, measuring the amount of chloride in sweat [5]. High chloride levels indicate a CFTR protein defect [5]. Genetic testing can also be performed to identify specific mutations in the CFTR gene, which can help confirm the diagnosis and predict the severity of the disease [5][6].

**Management / Treatment**

There is currently no cure for cystic fibrosis [8]. However, a variety of treatments are available to manage symptoms, prevent complications, and improve the quality of life for individuals with CF [9]. These treatments include medications, airway clearance techniques, nutritional support, and other therapies [9][10].

*   **Medications:** Medicines can help maintain and improve lung function, fight infections, clear mucus, and improve protein function [9]. CFTR modulators can help faulty CFTR proteins work better but only work for certain gene mutations [9].
*   **Airway clearance therapy (ACT):** ACT techniques, such as chest physical therapy or using devices like oscillating PEP devices or airway clearance vests, help clear mucus from the airways [10][11].
*   **Enzyme and nutritional support:** Pancreatic enzyme replacement therapies help the body absorb food and nutrients [10]. Additional salt and nutritional supplements may also be needed [10].
*   **Physiotherapy:** A physiotherapist can teach breathing and posture techniques to help clear mucus from the lungs [12].

**Prognosis / Natural History**

The prognosis for individuals with cystic fibrosis has improved significantly over the past few decades [7]. Advances in treatment and management have led to increased life expectancy and improved quality of life [7]. The median predicted survival for children born with CF between 2019 and 2023 is approximately 61 years [2]. However, the prognosis can vary depending on the severity of the disease, the specific CFTR mutations, and the effectiveness of treatment [3].

**Epidemiology**

Cystic fibrosis is one of the most common life-shortening genetic diseases in Caucasians [5]. It affects approximately 1 in 2,500 to 3,500 newborns in the United States [5]. CF is less common in other ethnic groups [5]. Approximately 1,000 new cases of CF are diagnosed each year [5].

**History / Nomenclature**

The characteristics of cystic fibrosis have been recognized for centuries, but the disease was not formally described as a distinct clinical entity until the 1930s [5]. In 1938, Dr. Dorothy Andersen published a landmark paper describing the clinical and pathological features of CF, which she initially termed "cystic fibrosis of the pancreas" [5]. The CFTR gene was identified in 1989, marking a major breakthrough in understanding the molecular basis of the disease [5].

**Research & Trials**

Ongoing research efforts are focused on developing new and improved therapies for cystic fibrosis, including gene therapy, personalized medicine approaches, and novel CFTR modulators [7]. Clinical trials are essential for evaluating the safety and effectiveness of these new treatments [7]. Individuals with CF and their families are encouraged to participate in research studies to help advance our understanding of the disease and improve patient outcomes [7].

**Uncertainties & Controversies**

While significant progress has been made in treating cystic fibrosis, uncertainties and controversies remain. The effectiveness of CFTR modulator therapies varies depending on the specific CFTR mutations, and these therapies are not effective for all individuals with CF [9]. The long-term effects of CFTR modulators are still being studied. There is also ongoing debate about the optimal strategies for managing specific complications of CF, such as CF-related diabetes and liver disease.
