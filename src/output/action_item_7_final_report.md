# Understanding Duchenne Muscular Dystrophy: Genetics, Clinical Progression, and Therapeutic Advances

This article is for education only and does not replace advice from your clinician.

## Lead
Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) is a severe, progressive X‑linked neuromuscular disorder characterized by loss of dystrophin, leading to weakening and wasting of skeletal and cardiac muscle.[1] It typically presents in early childhood with delayed motor milestones and progressive muscle loss that often leads to loss of ambulation in adolescence and shortened life expectancy.[1][41]

## Names and classification
Duchenne muscular dystrophy is classified among the dystrophinopathies and is caused by pathogenic variants in the DMD gene.[1][2] Authoritative disease resources that index DMD include GeneReviews,[1] Orphanet,[25] and the Genetic and Rare Diseases Information Center (GARD).[27]

## Epidemiology
Available sources agree that DMD usually begins in early childhood and predominantly affects males because of X‑linked inheritance.[1][27] Precise global prevalence or incidence figures were not consistently provided in the retrieved inputs, so uncertainty remains about exact population rates based on the retrieved documents.

## Signs and symptoms
The characteristic clinical course is early‑onset, progressive muscle wasting and weakness with gradual loss of motor abilities.[1] Typical early findings include delayed motor milestones and difficulties with running and climbing; most affected children lose independent ambulation by the early teens.[1] Cardiac involvement, evolving to dilated cardiomyopathy and arrhythmias, is common and becomes a major determinant of morbidity and mortality as patients age.[1][3] Respiratory muscle weakness also develops with disease progression, increasing risk of respiratory failure in later stages.[1]

Common clinical features reported across cohorts:
- Progressive proximal muscle weakness and wasting (childhood onset).  
- Loss of ambulation during adolescence.  
- Progressive cardiomyopathy and respiratory decline.[1][3]

## Pathophysiology and genetics
DMD results from mutations in the DMD gene that disrupt production or function of dystrophin, a protein that stabilizes muscle cell membranes and participates in signaling; absence or deficiency of dystrophin causes muscle fiber instability and degeneration.[1][2] Mutation types reported across large databases and cohort studies include large deletions, duplications, and a spectrum of small mutations (nonsense, splice‑site, small indels, and missense variants).[3][4] Large rearrangements are the majority of mutations (roughly two‑thirds deletions and about one‑tenth duplications), with the remaining ~20% composed of small-scale mutations; common deletion hotspots are clustered around exons roughly corresponding to the proximal (exons ≈2–20) and distal (exons ≈44–55) regions of the gene.[3][4]

Inheritance is X‑linked, and a substantial fraction of cases arise from de novo mutations, making molecular genetic testing central to diagnosis, carrier detection, and family counseling.[3]

## Diagnosis
Genetic testing is the critical diagnostic approach; recommended methods include copy‑number assays (for example, MLPA) to detect exon deletions/duplications and sequencing to detect small nucleotide variants, with RNA or muscle analysis reserved for unresolved cases.[4] Clinical evaluation—including history, examination, and measurement of creatine kinase—remains important, and muscle biopsy may be used selectively when genetic testing is nondiagnostic or to assess dystrophin protein directly.[4] Precise genotype information is frequently required for prognostic interpretation and for determining eligibility for mutation‑specific therapies and clinical trials.[4][13]

## Management
Multidisciplinary care addressing neuromuscular, cardiac, respiratory, rehabilitation, orthopedic, and psychosocial needs forms the foundation of management in DMD and is recommended by contemporary care guidelines.[36] Cardiac screening and proactive cardioprotective therapy (for example, early use of ACE inhibitors or beta‑blockers as indicated) are emphasized because cardiomyopathy is a leading cause of late morbidity and mortality.[35][36]

Mutation‑targeted therapies exist for specific genetic subgroups (for example, exon‑skipping antisense oligonucleotides) and require confirmed DMD gene mutation testing to determine eligibility.[12][36] Gene‑replacement approaches (micro‑dystrophin) and other experimental therapies are described in regulatory and trial documents as mutation‑or mechanism‑directed interventions, but evidence about their long‑term effects on cardiac and respiratory outcomes is limited in the retrieved sources.[6][46]

## Prognosis and natural history
DMD is progressive: skeletal muscle weakness advances over childhood and adolescence, while cardiac and respiratory complications accumulate over time and become the principal causes of life‑threatening complications.[1][41] In large cohort data, median age at death has been reported near 19.5 years in some historic series,[41] while more recent cohorts and registry‑based summaries indicate extended survival into the late twenties and beyond in patients receiving modern multidisciplinary and ventilatory support, with median life expectancy estimates around 28 years in some analyses.[27][41] The retrieved longitudinal studies show that worsening muscle function often parallels declining cardiac function, linking musculoskeletal progression with cardiopulmonary outcomes.[40][41]

## Research and clinical trials
Major research directions include mutation‑specific molecular therapies (exon skipping, stop‑codon readthrough), micro‑dystrophin gene‑replacement approaches (including approved and investigational products such as Elevidys and multiple micro‑dystrophin programs), and emerging agents targeting fibrosis, inflammation, or cardiomyocyte function.[6][18][46] Many trials rely on surrogate endpoints such as dystrophin expression or timed motor function tests; regulatory approvals for some therapies have been granted based on these surrogates rather than demonstrated long‑term improvement in cardiac or respiratory morbidity or overall survival.[48][46]

Selected investigational programs and trial designs cited in retrieved registries include SGT‑003 (gene therapy with explicit genetic eligibility criteria) and multiple micro‑dystrophin trials that require molecular confirmation of DMD for enrollment, underscoring the centrality of genetic diagnosis for trial participation.[18][19] The retrieved materials note ongoing efforts to incorporate cardiac imaging and functional cardiopulmonary endpoints into longer‑term follow‑up, but they also emphasize that robust evidence on durable cardiorespiratory benefit from novel molecular therapies remains limited.[18][46]

## What is not known
Long‑term effects of mutation‑targeted and gene therapies on cardiac and respiratory outcomes remain uncertain in the retrieved literature, as most pivotal studies were conducted in younger, ambulatory populations and used surrogate endpoints rather than late cardiopulmonary morbidity or survival.[46] True global prevalence estimates were not consistently reported in the provided sources, and outcome data beyond surrogate markers (for example, on life expectancy and quality of life after novel therapies) remain incomplete based on the retrieved documents.
