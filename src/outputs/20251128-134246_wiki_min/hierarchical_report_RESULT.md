**1. Quick Facts**

*   Duchenne Muscular Dystrophy (DMD) is a genetic disorder characterized by progressive muscle weakness and degeneration [2].
*   It primarily affects males and is caused by mutations in the *dystrophin* gene [2].
*   The prevalence of DMD is approximately 1 in 3,500 to 5,000 male births [1, 2].
*   Symptoms typically appear in early childhood, between 2 and 3 years of age [2].
*   DMD leads to significant disability and reduced life expectancy, although advances in care have improved outcomes [5].

**2. What is Duchenne Muscular Dystrophy (DMD)?**

Duchenne Muscular Dystrophy (DMD) is a genetic disorder that causes progressive muscle weakness and loss of muscle mass. It is primarily seen in males. The condition is caused by a defect in the *dystrophin* gene. Dystrophin is a protein that acts like "glue" to hold muscle fibers together, protecting them from injury when muscles contract [2, 3]. Without enough dystrophin, muscle cells become damaged and weaken over time.

**3. Signs and Symptoms**

The signs and symptoms of DMD typically appear in early childhood [2]. They can vary in severity but generally include:

*   Delayed motor milestones, such as difficulty sitting, standing, or walking [2].
*   Difficulty rising from the floor (Gower's sign), which involves using hands and arms to "walk up" the legs [2].
*   Frequent falls [2].
*   Muscle weakness that starts in the hips and thighs before progressing to the shoulders, arms, and other areas [2].
*   Learning disabilities may occur in some cases [1].
*   Cardiomyopathy (weakening of the heart muscle) and respiratory difficulties can develop as the disease progresses [1].

**4. What Causes DMD?**

DMD is caused by mutations in the *dystrophin* gene (DMD gene), which is located on the X chromosome [2]. Because males have only one X chromosome, a mutation in this gene usually results in DMD. Females have two X chromosomes, so a mutation on one X chromosome can often be compensated for by the normal gene on the other X chromosome. DMD follows an X-linked recessive inheritance pattern, meaning that mothers can be carriers of the mutated gene and pass it on to their sons [2, 8]. In some cases, DMD can result from a *de novo* mutation, meaning a new mutation that occurs spontaneously [2].

**5. How Does DMD Affect the Body?**

The lack of dystrophin in DMD leads to muscle cell damage and progressive muscle weakness [1, 10, 11, 14]. This affects skeletal muscles (muscles used for movement), cardiac muscle (heart muscle), and respiratory muscles (muscles used for breathing). Over time, the damage to skeletal muscles leads to increasing difficulty with movement and mobility. Cardiomyopathy can lead to heart failure, and respiratory muscle weakness can cause breathing problems and respiratory failure [1]. Scoliosis (curvature of the spine) is also a common complication [1].

**6. How is DMD Diagnosed?**

DMD is diagnosed through a combination of clinical evaluation, blood tests, genetic testing, and sometimes muscle biopsy [2, 3]. The diagnostic process typically includes:

*   Clinical evaluation: Assessing muscle weakness, motor skills, and other symptoms.
*   Creatine kinase (CK) blood test: Elevated CK levels in the blood indicate muscle damage [3].
*   Genetic testing: Analyzing a blood sample to identify mutations in the *dystrophin* gene [2].
*   Muscle biopsy: Taking a small sample of muscle tissue for examination under a microscope, which can be used if genetic testing is inconclusive.

**7. How is DMD Treated?**

There is currently no cure for DMD, but treatments are available to help manage the symptoms and slow the progression of the disease [3, 4, 6]. Treatment options include:

*   Corticosteroids (prednisone, deflazacort): These medications can help slow muscle degeneration and improve muscle strength [3, 7, 12, 13].
*   Exon-skipping therapies (e.g., eteplirsen, golodirsen, viltolarsen): These drugs are designed to help the body produce a shorter, but still functional, version of dystrophin [6].
*   Ataluren: This medication is used for individuals with specific types of mutations that cause the body to stop producing dystrophin prematurely.
*   Physical therapy and occupational therapy: These therapies help maintain muscle strength and flexibility, prevent contractures, and improve mobility.
*   Respiratory support: Non-invasive ventilation (e.g., BiPAP) may be needed to assist with breathing as the respiratory muscles weaken.
*   Cardiac management: Medications such as ACE inhibitors and beta-blockers are used to manage cardiomyopathy.
*   Nutritional support: Maintaining a healthy diet and weight is important.
*   Surgical interventions: Surgery may be needed to correct scoliosis or other complications.
*   Delandistrogene moxeparvovec (gene therapy): This gene therapy aims to deliver a functional dystrophin gene to muscle cells [6].

**8. Living with DMD**

Living with DMD presents significant challenges for individuals and their families [5, 15, 16]. Management strategies include:

*   Mobility aids: Wheelchairs, walkers, and other assistive devices can help with mobility.
*   Home modifications: Modifying the home to make it more accessible, such as installing ramps and grab bars.
*   Assistive technology: Using technology to assist with communication, learning, and daily tasks.
*   Support groups and patient advocacy organizations: Connecting with other families and individuals affected by DMD can provide emotional support and practical advice [15, 16].
*   Psychological support and counseling: Addressing the emotional and psychological challenges of living with a chronic illness.

**9. What is the Outlook for People with DMD?**

The prognosis for individuals with DMD has improved significantly over the past few decades due to advances in treatment and care [5]. While DMD is still a progressive disease with significant challenges, early intervention and comprehensive management can help improve quality of life and extend life expectancy [5].

**10. How Common is DMD?**

DMD affects approximately 1 in 3,500 to 5,000 male births [1, 2].

**11. Research and Future Treatments**

Ongoing research efforts are focused on developing new and more effective treatments for DMD [3, 5, 6]. These include:

*   Gene therapy: Delivering a functional dystrophin gene to muscle cells [5].
*   Exon skipping: Modifying the way the body reads the dystrophin gene to produce a shorter, but still functional, protein [6].
*   Cell-based therapies: Using stem cells or other cells to repair or replace damaged muscle tissue.
*   Myostatin inhibitors: Blocking myostatin, a protein that inhibits muscle growth.
*   Stop codon read-through therapies: Allowing the body to continue reading the dystrophin gene past a premature stop codon.
*   New drug development: Identifying and testing new drugs that can improve muscle function and slow disease progression.

It is important to note that these treatments are still in the experimental stages and may not lead to immediate cures [3, 5, 6].

**12. History**

*   DMD was first described in detail by the French neurologist Guillaume Benjamin Amand Duchenne in the 1860s.

**13. Glossary of Terms**

*   **Dystrophin:** A protein that is essential for maintaining the structure and function of muscle cells.
*   **Mutation:** A change in the DNA sequence of a gene.
*   **X-linked recessive:** A pattern of inheritance in which a gene is located on the X chromosome and a mutation is more likely to cause disease in males.
*   **Creatine kinase (CK):** An enzyme found in muscle tissue; elevated levels in the blood indicate muscle damage.
*   **Cardiomyopathy:** Weakening of the heart muscle.
*   **Respiratory failure:** A condition in which the lungs cannot adequately provide oxygen to the body or remove carbon dioxide.
*   **Exon skipping:** A therapeutic approach that modifies the way the body reads the dystrophin gene to produce a shorter, but still functional, protein.
*   **Gene therapy:** A therapeutic approach that involves delivering a functional gene to cells to correct a genetic defect.
