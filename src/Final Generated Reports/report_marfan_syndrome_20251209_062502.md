## Marfan Syndrome Overview

Marfan syndrome (MFS) is a genetic disorder that affects the body's connective tissue [1, 2]. Connective tissue is vital because it holds all the body's cells, organs, and tissues together [3]. This tissue is composed of proteins, one of which is fibrillin-1, and plays a key role in growth and development [1, 3]. Marfan syndrome can impact various parts of the body, including the heart, blood vessels, bones, joints, and eyes [1, 3].

The condition presents differently in each individual, with symptoms varying in severity and the age of onset [1]. It affects approximately 1 in 5,000 people across all races and ethnic groups [1, 2]. While often inherited, about 25% of cases arise from a spontaneous new gene change [1, 5]. Treatment focuses on managing symptoms and preventing complications, allowing many individuals with Marfan syndrome to live long and fulfilling lives [9].

### Overview / Definition

Marfan syndrome is a genetic condition that impacts the body's connective tissue [1, 2]. Connective tissue is made up of proteins, including fibrillin-1, and provides structure and support to cells, organs, and tissues [1, 3]. This tissue is crucial for proper growth and development [3, 4]. Marfan syndrome exhibits variable expression, meaning that the severity and presentation of symptoms can differ significantly among affected individuals [1].

### Signs & Symptoms

Individuals with Marfan syndrome may experience a range of signs and symptoms. These can include disproportionately long arms, legs, fingers, and toes [1, 2]. A tall and thin body type is also common [1, 2]. Other symptoms can include loose joints [6], a sunken or protruding chest [1, 7], stretch marks even without weight changes [1], and an abnormal curvature of the spine [2]. The presence and severity of these symptoms can vary greatly from person to person [1].

### Genetics & Pathophysiology

Marfan syndrome is typically inherited from a parent [1]. There is a 50% chance that a parent with Marfan syndrome will pass the affected gene on to each child [1]. However, approximately 25% of cases result from a spontaneous new gene change of unknown cause [1, 5]. The condition arises due to mutations in the *FBN1* gene, which provides instructions for making fibrillin-1 [3, 9].

### Diagnosis

Diagnosing Marfan syndrome involves a thorough evaluation that includes reviewing the individual's medical and family history, conducting a physical examination, and performing diagnostic testing [1, 9]. Doctors look for characteristic signs of the syndrome and inquire about family members with related health issues [1]. Diagnostic tests may include echocardiograms to assess the heart and genetic testing to identify mutations in the *FBN1* gene [9]. The Ghent nosology is also a set of diagnostic criteria doctors use.

### Management / Treatment

Currently, there is no cure for Marfan syndrome [2, 9]. Treatment is aimed at managing the symptoms and preventing potential complications [2, 9]. Treatment options include medications such as beta-blockers or angiotensin receptor blockers to manage heart problems [9]. Surgery may be necessary to repair aortic aneurysms, correct skeletal abnormalities, or address eye complications [2, 9]. Lifestyle modifications, such as avoiding certain sports that place excessive stress on the heart, are also recommended [9].

### Prognosis / Natural History

With appropriate treatment and management, individuals with Marfan syndrome can lead long and fulfilling lives [9]. Medical advances have significantly improved life expectancy for those with the condition [9]. Regular monitoring and adherence to treatment plans are essential for maintaining good health and preventing complications [1].

### Epidemiology

Marfan syndrome affects approximately 1 in 5,000 people [1, 2]. It is found in all races and ethnic groups [1, 2]. The condition affects males and females equally.

### Uncertainties & Controversies

The variable expression of Marfan syndrome makes diagnosis challenging, particularly in individuals with milder symptoms. The precise mechanisms by which *FBN1* mutations lead to specific manifestations of the disease are still being investigated.
