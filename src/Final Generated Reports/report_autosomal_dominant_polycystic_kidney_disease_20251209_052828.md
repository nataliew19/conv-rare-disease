## Autosomal Dominant Polycystic Kidney Disease

### Lead / Summary
Autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD) is a prevalent genetic disorder characterized by the development of numerous cysts within the kidneys [1][2]. Affecting approximately 1 in 400 to 1 in 1,000 individuals, it stands as the most common inherited kidney disease [1]. In Europe, the estimated prevalence is 1 in 2,500 [2]. These cysts progressively enlarge, leading to kidney damage and eventual kidney failure [1]. ADPKD is the fourth leading cause of kidney failure worldwide [3].

The condition is caused by mutations in the *PKD1* and *PKD2* genes, which are vital for normal kidney development and function [2][4]. Although ADPKD primarily affects the kidneys, it can also involve other organs, such as the liver and cardiovascular system [2][5]. Treatment strategies focus on managing symptoms and slowing the progression of kidney failure [1].

### Overview / Definition
Autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD) is a genetic disorder distinguished by the formation of cysts in the kidneys [1][2]. This inherited condition is the most common of its kind, affecting a significant portion of the population [1]. The prevalence of ADPKD in Europe is estimated to be 1 in 2,500 [2]. These cysts can progressively enlarge and impair kidney function, ultimately leading to kidney failure [1]. The genetic basis of ADPKD lies in mutations of the *PKD1* and *PKD2* genes [2][4].

### Signs & Symptoms
Diagnosis of ADPKD typically occurs between the ages of 30 and 50, when noticeable signs and symptoms begin to emerge [1]. Common symptoms include high blood pressure (hypertension), flank pain (pain on the sides of the body between the last rib and the hip), blood in the urine (hematuria), and a gradual decline in kidney function (kidney insufficiency) [2]. Most individuals with ADPKD experience pain, high blood pressure, and kidney failure at some point in their lives [1]. Pain is a frequent complication, often resulting from cysts in the kidneys or liver [1].

### Genetics & Pathophysiology
ADPKD is primarily caused by mutations in two genes: *PKD1* and *PKD2* [2][4]. Approximately 85% of cases are linked to mutations in the *PKD1* gene, while the remaining cases involve mutations in the *PKD2* gene [2][4]. These genes are essential for producing proteins involved in normal kidney development, organization, and function [4]. The enlargement of cysts in the kidneys leads to kidney damage and eventual kidney failure [1].

### Management / Treatment
The primary focus of ADPKD treatment is to slow the progression of kidney failure and manage the associated features of the disease [1]. This includes managing high blood pressure, treating kidney infections, addressing kidney stones, and alleviating flank or abdominal pain [1].

### Prognosis / Natural History
ADPKD often leads to kidney failure, typically by the sixth decade of life [1]. It is estimated that about 50% of individuals with ADPKD will experience kidney failure by the age of 70 [3].

### Epidemiology
ADPKD affects 1 in every 400 to 1,000 people and is the most common kidney disorder passed down through family members [1]. It is estimated to have a prevalence of 1 in 2,500 in Europe [2]. ADPKD is the fourth leading cause of kidney failure globally [3].

### Uncertainties & Controversies
The specific rate of disease progression in ADPKD can vary widely among individuals, making it challenging to predict the long-term outcomes for a given patient. While certain genetic mutations are more commonly associated with ADPKD, the precise relationship between genotype and phenotype remains an area of ongoing research. Additionally, the optimal strategies for managing pain and other complications associated with ADPKD are still debated among healthcare professionals.
