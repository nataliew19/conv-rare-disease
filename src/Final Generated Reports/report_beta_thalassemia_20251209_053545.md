## Beta Thalassemia

### Lead / Summary

Beta thalassemia is an inherited blood disorder characterized by the body's decreased ability to produce hemoglobin [1][2]. Hemoglobin, a protein found in red blood cells, is essential for carrying oxygen throughout the body [1]. The severity of beta thalassemia varies, with some individuals experiencing mild or no symptoms, while others require regular blood transfusions [2]. Understanding the different types, causes, and treatments of beta thalassemia is crucial for effective management and improved quality of life for affected individuals.

This condition arises due to mutations in the *HBB* gene, which provides instructions for making beta-globin, a vital component of hemoglobin [3]. The specific mutation dictates the severity of the disease, leading to varying degrees of anemia and associated complications [3]. While there is currently no universal cure, treatments like blood transfusions, iron chelation, and bone marrow transplantation can significantly improve the prognosis for many patients [5, 8]. Research into gene therapy offers hope for future curative options [8].

### Overview / Definition

Beta thalassemia is an inherited blood disorder in which the body produces reduced levels of functional hemoglobin [1][2]. Hemoglobin is an iron-rich protein in red blood cells responsible for carrying oxygen from the lungs to all parts of the body [1]. In beta thalassemia, the reduced production of beta-globin, a subunit of hemoglobin, leads to anemia [3]. Think of hemoglobin as the delivery trucks that carry oxygen. When there aren't enough trucks or the trucks aren't working correctly, the body doesn't get enough oxygen.

### Signs & Symptoms

The signs and symptoms of beta thalassemia vary depending on the type and severity of the condition [1, 2]. Individuals with beta thalassemia minor often have no symptoms [6]. Beta thalassemia intermedia is characterized by moderate anemia, which can lead to fatigue, paleness, slow growth, and bone problems [1, 2]. Beta thalassemia major, also known as Cooley's anemia, presents with severe anemia symptoms that typically appear within the first two years of life [2, 4]. These symptoms include failure to thrive, jaundice (yellowing of the skin and eyes), and an enlarged spleen [2, 4]. General symptoms of anemia, regardless of the type of beta thalassemia, can include fatigue, weakness, and shortness of breath [1].

### Genetics & Pathophysiology

Beta thalassemia is caused by mutations in the *HBB* gene, which is responsible for providing instructions for making beta-globin [3]. The *HBB* gene is located on chromosome 11 [3]. These mutations result in a reduced or absent production of beta-globin, a crucial component of hemoglobin [3]. The severity of beta thalassemia depends on the specific mutation in the *HBB* gene and how it affects beta-globin production [3]. Beta thalassemia is inherited from parents [4]. Individuals who inherit one mutated gene are carriers (beta thalassemia minor), while those who inherit two mutated genes have beta thalassemia major or intermedia, depending on the specific mutations [3].

### Diagnosis

The diagnosis of beta thalassemia involves a clinical evaluation, specialized tests, and identification of characteristic symptoms [4]. Initial symptoms, such as failure to thrive and a swollen abdomen, may raise suspicion for beta thalassemia major [4]. A complete blood count (CBC) is performed to assess red blood cell levels, size, and shape [4]. Hemoglobin electrophoresis is used to identify and quantify the different types of hemoglobin in the blood, helping to confirm the diagnosis [4]. Genetic testing can also be performed to identify specific *HBB* gene mutations [4].

### Management / Treatment

Treatment for beta thalassemia depends on the type and severity of the condition [5]. Beta thalassemia minor usually requires no specific treatment [6]. Beta thalassemia major is typically treated with regular blood transfusions to maintain adequate hemoglobin levels [5]. Because frequent transfusions can lead to iron overload, iron chelation therapy is also necessary to remove excess iron from the body [7]. Bone marrow transplantation (stem cell transplant) is a potential curative option for some individuals with beta thalassemia major [8]. Gene therapy is also being investigated as a potential future treatment option [8].

### Prognosis / Natural History

The prognosis for individuals with beta thalassemia varies depending on the type and severity of the condition [1, 2]. Individuals with beta thalassemia minor typically have a normal life expectancy and may not experience any significant health problems [6]. The prognosis for individuals with beta thalassemia major has improved significantly with advancements in treatment, such as regular blood transfusions and iron chelation therapy [5, 7]. Bone marrow transplantation can potentially cure the disease, but it carries risks [8]. Gene therapy holds promise for future curative options [8].

### Epidemiology

Beta thalassemia is most common in people of Mediterranean, Middle Eastern, South Asian, and Southeast Asian descent [1]. The exact prevalence of beta thalassemia varies depending on the population [2]. Beta thalassemia minor (carrier status) is more common than beta thalassemia major [1].

### History / Nomenclature

Thalassemia was first described in the 1920s by Thomas Cooley and Pearl Lee, who observed severe anemia in children of Mediterranean descent [2]. The term "thalassemia" comes from the Greek word "thalassa," meaning sea, referring to the Mediterranean Sea, where the condition is most prevalent [2]. Beta thalassemia specifically refers to the form of thalassemia that affects the production of beta-globin [3]. Cooley's anemia is another name for beta thalassemia major, named after one of the physicians who first described the condition [1].

### Research & Trials

Current research efforts are focused on developing new and improved treatments for beta thalassemia [8]. Gene therapy aims to correct the mutated *HBB* gene, allowing the body to produce normal beta-globin [8]. Researchers are also working on developing new chelation therapies to more effectively manage iron overload [7]. Improved bone marrow transplantation techniques are also being investigated to increase the success rate and reduce the risks associated with this procedure [8]. Clinical trials are ongoing to evaluate the safety and efficacy of these new treatments [8].

### Uncertainties & Controversies

While significant progress has been made in the treatment of beta thalassemia, some uncertainties and controversies remain. The long-term effects of chronic blood transfusions and iron chelation therapy are still being studied [7]. The optimal timing and approach for bone marrow transplantation are also subjects of ongoing debate [8]. The high cost and limited accessibility of gene therapy remain significant challenges [8].
