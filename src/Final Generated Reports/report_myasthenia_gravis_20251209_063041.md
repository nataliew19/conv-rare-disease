## Myasthenia Gravis

**Lead / Summary**

Myasthenia gravis (MG) is a chronic autoimmune neuromuscular disorder characterized by fluctuating muscle weakness and fatigue [1][2]. The condition arises from a disruption in the communication between nerves and muscles, caused by antibodies that attack the body’s own acetylcholine receptors [2]. Although there is no cure for MG, treatments are available to help manage symptoms and improve quality of life [1]. MG is not contagious or hereditary [2].

The symptoms of MG can vary widely, but often include drooping eyelids, double vision, and weakness in the limbs and facial muscles [1]. Diagnosis typically involves a combination of physical and neurological examinations, blood tests to detect specific antibodies, and other tests to assess neuromuscular function [2]. Treatment strategies include medications to enhance muscle strength, immunosuppressant drugs to reduce the autoimmune response, and, in some cases, surgical removal of the thymus gland [1]. Support groups play a crucial role in helping individuals with myasthenia gravis support each other, learn more about managing MG, and better understand the disease and how to navigate its challenges [5].

**Overview / Definition**

Myasthenia gravis (MG) is a chronic autoimmune neuromuscular disease that leads to fluctuating muscle weakness and fatigue [1][2]. The term "myasthenia gravis" itself means "grave muscle weakness." The underlying cause of this weakness is a breakdown in the normal communication between nerves and muscles [2]. This communication breakdown occurs at the neuromuscular junction, where nerve impulses trigger muscle contraction.

**Signs & Symptoms**

The signs and symptoms of MG vary from person to person and fluctuate in severity [2]. Common symptoms include weakness of the eye muscles (ocular myasthenia), leading to drooping of one or both eyelids (ptosis) and blurred or double vision (diplopia) [1]. Weakness can also affect the arms, hands, fingers, legs, and neck [1]. Facial muscle involvement can result in a mask-like appearance, and a smile may appear more like a snarl [2]. Other symptoms can include difficulty swallowing (dysphagia) or pronouncing words (dysarthria) [2]. These symptoms tend to worsen with activity and improve with rest [2]. Flare-ups and remissions are characteristic of the disease course [2].

**Genetics & Pathophysiology**

MG is an autoimmune disease, meaning that the body's immune system mistakenly attacks its own tissues [2]. In MG, the immune system produces antibodies that target the acetylcholine receptors (AChRs) at the neuromuscular junction [3]. Acetylcholine (ACh) is a neurotransmitter that plays a critical role in transmitting signals between nerves and muscles. When ACh is released by a nerve ending, it binds to AChRs on the muscle cell, triggering muscle contraction. In MG, the antibodies block, alter, or destroy these AChRs, preventing ACh from effectively binding and initiating muscle contraction. This leads to muscle weakness and fatigue [3]. The thymus gland, which plays a role in immune system development, is also implicated in MG. Abnormalities of the thymus gland are common in individuals with MG, and thymectomy (surgical removal of the thymus) is a treatment option [1].

**Diagnosis**

Diagnosing MG involves a combination of clinical evaluation and diagnostic testing [2]. The diagnostic process begins with a thorough physical and neurological examination to assess muscle strength, reflexes, and other neurological functions [2]. Blood tests are performed to detect the presence of antibodies against acetylcholine receptors (AChR antibodies) [2]. Another blood test looks for antibodies against muscle-specific kinase (MuSK), another protein involved in neuromuscular junction function [3]. The edrophonium (Tensilon) test involves administering edrophonium, a medication that temporarily improves muscle strength in individuals with MG [2]. Single-fiber electromyography (SFEMG) is a sensitive test used to detect impaired neuromuscular transmission [3].

**Management / Treatment**

While there is no cure for MG [2], various treatment options are available to manage symptoms and improve quality of life [1]. Medications commonly used to improve muscle strength include cholinesterase inhibitors, such as pyridostigmine, which increase the amount of acetylcholine available at the neuromuscular junction [2]. Immunosuppressant medications, such as corticosteroids (e.g., prednisone), azathioprine, mycophenolate mofetil and rituximab are used to reduce the autoimmune attack [1]. Thymectomy, surgical removal of the thymus gland, is another treatment option, particularly for patients with thymus abnormalities [1]. Plasmapheresis and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) are treatments used during myasthenic crisis [1].

**Prognosis / Natural History**

The prognosis for individuals with MG is generally good, with most people having a normal life expectancy [4]. Symptoms tend to peak in severity within one to three years of initial diagnosis [4]. Some individuals may experience remissions, which can be temporary or permanent [4]. With appropriate treatment and management, many people with MG can lead active and fulfilling lives [4].

**Epidemiology**

MG can affect anyone at any age, although it is more frequently observed in young women (ages 20 and 30) and older men (aged 50 and older) [2]. Globally, approximately 150 to 200 out of every million people have myasthenia gravis [1].

**History / Nomenclature**

The first clinical description of myasthenia gravis is generally attributed to Thomas Willis in 1672. However, it wasn't until the late 19th century that more detailed accounts emerged. In 1895, Wilhelm Erb and Samuel Goldflam independently described the key features of the disease, including muscle weakness and fatigue. The term "myasthenia gravis pseudoparalytica" was coined by Oppenheim. The autoimmune nature of the disease was not fully understood until much later, with the identification of acetylcholine receptor antibodies in the 1970s.

**Research & Trials**

Ongoing research efforts are focused on better understanding the underlying causes of MG and developing new and more effective treatments [1]. This includes research into the role of the thymus gland, the specific immune mechanisms involved in the autoimmune attack, and the development of novel therapeutic agents. Clinical trials are evaluating the safety and efficacy of new medications and treatment strategies for MG [1].

**Uncertainties & Controversies**

While significant progress has been made in understanding and treating MG, some uncertainties and controversies remain. The precise role of the thymus gland in the pathogenesis of MG is not fully elucidated [3]. The optimal timing and approach to thymectomy are also subjects of ongoing debate. The long-term efficacy and safety of different immunosuppressant medications are continuously being evaluated [3]. The specific triggers that lead to myasthenic crises are not always identifiable [3].
