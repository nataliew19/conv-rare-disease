## Phenylketonuria (PKU)

**Lead / Summary**

Phenylketonuria (PKU) is a rare genetic disorder present from birth [1]. It is classified as an inborn error of metabolism [2]. The primary characteristic of PKU is the body's inability to properly process the amino acid phenylalanine [1, 2]. This deficiency is due to the absence or malfunction of an enzyme called phenylalanine hydroxylase (PAH) [3]. If left untreated, the buildup of phenylalanine can lead to serious health consequences, including intellectual disability [1, 4].

Newborns are screened for PKU in the United States [1]. Early diagnosis and management through dietary restrictions and medication are crucial in preventing complications [7]. While there is no cure for PKU [6], affected individuals can lead relatively normal lives with proper treatment and monitoring [7, 8]. This report provides a comprehensive overview of PKU, covering its causes, symptoms, diagnosis, treatment, and ongoing research efforts.

**Overview / Definition**

Phenylketonuria (PKU) is an inherited metabolic disorder [2]. It occurs when the body cannot properly break down phenylalanine, an amino acid found in protein-containing foods and some artificial sweeteners [3]. The breakdown of phenylalanine requires the enzyme phenylalanine hydroxylase (PAH) [3]. In individuals with PKU, a deficiency or absence of PAH results in the accumulation of phenylalanine in the blood and brain [1, 4]. High levels of phenylalanine are toxic and can cause neurological damage [1, 4].

**Signs & Symptoms**

Newborns with PKU typically appear healthy at birth [1]. However, without treatment, symptoms develop gradually over several months, usually within three to six months [1]. One of the early signs is developmental delays [1]. Other symptoms that can manifest within the first year include a musty odor in the breath, sweat, or urine [1]. Untreated individuals may also experience seizures, delayed development, behavioral problems, and psychiatric disorders [4]. Children with classic PKU often have lighter skin and hair compared to other family members and may develop skin disorders such as eczema [4].

**Genetics & Pathophysiology**

PKU is caused by mutations in the *PAH* gene [1]. This gene contains the instructions for producing the enzyme phenylalanine hydroxylase (PAH) [1]. These mutations disrupt the function of the PAH enzyme, leading to a buildup of phenylalanine in the body [1, 4]. PKU follows an autosomal recessive inheritance pattern [1]. This means that an individual must inherit two copies of the mutated gene, one from each parent, to develop the condition [1]. Parents are typically carriers of the gene, meaning they have one copy of the mutated gene and one normal copy, and do not exhibit symptoms of PKU themselves [1, 2].

**Diagnosis**

All newborns in the United States are screened for PKU shortly after birth as part of routine newborn screening programs [1]. This screening involves testing a small blood sample to detect elevated levels of phenylalanine [1]. If the initial screening indicates high phenylalanine levels, further confirmatory testing is performed [5]. This includes gene sequencing to identify pathogenic variants in the *PAH* gene, confirming the diagnosis of PKU [5].

**Management / Treatment**

There is currently no cure for PKU [6]. However, early and consistent treatment is essential to prevent serious complications, such as intellectual disabilities [7]. The primary treatment for PKU is a special diet that is low in phenylalanine [1, 7, 8]. This requires restricting the intake of high-protein foods, such as meat, fish, eggs, and dairy products [7]. Phenylalanine-free amino acid supplements are also used to ensure adequate protein intake [8]. In some cases, medication may be prescribed to help lower phenylalanine levels.

**Prognosis / Natural History**

Without treatment, PKU leads to a buildup of phenylalanine, which causes significant neurological damage, resulting in intellectual disability, seizures, and other neurological problems [1, 4]. However, with early diagnosis and consistent adherence to a low-phenylalanine diet, individuals with PKU can lead relatively normal lives with typical cognitive development [7]. Lifelong dietary management and monitoring of phenylalanine levels are necessary to maintain optimal health [7, 8].

**Epidemiology**

PKU is a rare disorder [1]. The exact incidence varies among different populations, but it is estimated to affect approximately 1 in 10,000 to 1 in 15,000 newborns in the United States [2].

**History / Nomenclature**

Phenylketonuria was first discovered in 1934 by Dr. Asbjørn Følling in Norway [6]. He identified the presence of phenylketones in the urine of individuals with intellectual disabilities, leading to the naming of the condition as phenylketonuria [6].

**Research & Trials**

Ongoing research efforts are focused on developing new and improved treatments for PKU. This includes research into gene therapy, which aims to correct the underlying genetic defect [5]. Enzyme replacement therapies are also being explored as a way to provide functional PAH enzyme [8]. These future treatments hold the potential to significantly improve the lives of individuals with PKU.

**Uncertainties & Controversies**

One area of uncertainty is the optimal level of phenylalanine control, particularly in adults with PKU [7]. There is ongoing debate about the long-term effects of slightly elevated phenylalanine levels and the intensity of dietary restrictions required to maintain optimal neurological function [7, 8]. Another controversy concerns the use of certain artificial sweeteners, such as aspartame, which contain phenylalanine. Individuals with PKU must carefully monitor their intake of these sweeteners to avoid elevating their phenylalanine levels [8].
