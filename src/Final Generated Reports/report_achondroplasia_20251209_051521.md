## Achondroplasia

**Lead / Summary**

Achondroplasia is the most common form of short-limbed dwarfism and a bone growth disorder [1][2]. It is a genetic condition characterized by impaired bone growth in the limbs, as well as abnormal growth in the spine and skull [3]. The condition is caused by a mutation in the *FGFR3* gene [3]. While there is no cure for achondroplasia, management focuses on treating and preventing potential complications [6].

Achondroplasia affects approximately 1 in 25,000 live births. Individuals with achondroplasia experience a range of symptoms, including short stature, disproportionately short limbs, and potential neurological and orthopedic complications. Diagnosis can occur prenatally or after birth through physical examination, X-rays, and genetic testing [4]. With appropriate medical care and support, individuals with achondroplasia can live full and productive lives [2][3]. This article provides an overview of achondroplasia for educational purposes and should not be used as a substitute for professional medical advice.

**Overview / Definition**

Achondroplasia is a genetic disorder primarily affecting bone and cartilage growth, making it the most common cause of dwarfism with rhizomelic shortening [1][2]. The term "dwarfism" refers to short stature [2]. Specifically, achondroplasia disrupts the conversion of cartilage to bone, especially in the long bones of the arms and legs, the spine, and the skull [3][4]. This leads to disproportionate growth, where individuals have an average-sized trunk but shortened limbs [2]. The condition is present from birth and impacts both skeletal development and overall stature.

**Signs & Symptoms**

Individuals with achondroplasia exhibit a number of characteristic physical features [2]. These include short stature, with adult males averaging 131 centimeters (4 feet, 4 inches) and adult females averaging 124 centimeters (4 feet, 1 inch) [2]. Other features include an average-sized trunk, short arms and legs (particularly in the upper arms and thighs), a large head (macrocephaly) with a prominent forehead, and limited range of motion at the elbows [2][3]. The fingers are typically short, and the ring and middle fingers may diverge, creating a trident-like appearance [2].

Additional common symptoms include shortened arms and legs (with the upper arms and thighs more affected than the forearms and lower legs), a curved lower spine (lordosis), small vertebral canals that may lead to spinal stenosis during adolescence, flat feet, extra space between the middle and ring fingers, poor muscle tone and loose joints, frequent ear infections that may lead to hearing loss, and delayed developmental milestones [3].

**Genetics & Pathophysiology**

Achondroplasia is caused by a mutation in the *FGFR3* (fibroblast growth factor receptor 3) gene [3]. The *FGFR3* gene provides instructions for making a protein that is involved in bone and brain tissue maintenance [3]. The mutation causes the FGFR3 protein to function abnormally, slowing down bone growth in the cartilage of the growth plate, which results in shorter and abnormally shaped bones [3]. Achondroplasia follows an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern [5]. However, approximately 80% of cases result from a spontaneous, new mutation in the *FGFR3* gene during early embryonic development, rather than being inherited from parents [3].

**Diagnosis**

Achondroplasia can be diagnosed prenatally through ultrasound, which may reveal shortened limbs and an enlarged head in the developing fetus [4]. After birth, diagnosis is typically confirmed based on a physical examination that reveals characteristic features [4]. X-rays can also be used to assess bone structure and confirm the diagnosis [4]. Genetic testing can be performed to identify the *FGFR3* mutation and provide a definitive diagnosis [4].

**Management / Treatment**

There is no cure for achondroplasia, so treatment focuses on managing symptoms and preventing or addressing potential complications [6]. Management strategies include weight management to reduce stress on joints, surgery to relieve pressure on the brain or correct craniocervical junction compression, surgery to remove adenoids and tonsils, use of a CPAP nasal mask for apnea, and ear tubes or antibiotics to prevent ear infections [6]. Regular monitoring of height, weight, and head circumference is crucial during infancy to track growth and development [6].

**Prognosis / Natural History**

While achondroplasia presents lifelong challenges, individuals with the condition can live full and productive lives [2][3]. Potential long-term complications include spinal stenosis and recurrent ear infections [3]. With appropriate medical management, life expectancy is generally normal [6]. Early intervention and supportive care are essential to optimize health and well-being.

**Epidemiology**

Achondroplasia affects approximately 1 in 25,000 live births. It is the most common form of short-limbed dwarfism [1][2]. The condition occurs in all racial and ethnic groups worldwide.

**History / Nomenclature**

The term "achondroplasia" comes from the Greek words "a-" meaning "without," "chondro-" meaning "cartilage," and "plasia" meaning "formation." The name reflects the historical understanding that the condition primarily involved defective cartilage formation.

**Research & Trials**

Ongoing research focuses on potential treatments for achondroplasia [4][6]. Areas of research include therapies targeting the FGFR3 pathway to promote bone growth and other interventions to address specific complications [4][6]. Clinical trials are underway to evaluate the safety and efficacy of these novel treatments.

**Uncertainties & Controversies**

While the genetic basis of achondroplasia is well-established, some variability exists in the severity and expression of symptoms. Further research is needed to fully understand the factors that contribute to this phenotypic variability. There is ongoing debate regarding the use of specific treatments, such as limb lengthening surgery, with varying perspectives on the risks and benefits.
