## Spinal Muscular Atrophy

**Lead / Summary**

Spinal Muscular Atrophy (SMA) is a genetic disorder impacting the nervous system and muscles, leading to muscle weakness and wasting [2][3]. The condition arises from the loss of motor neurons, which are crucial for controlling muscle movement [2][4]. It is important to note that SMA does not affect sensory nerves or a person's intellect [4]. While there is currently no cure for SMA, various treatments are available to help manage symptoms and enhance the quality of life for affected individuals [2][7].

SMA is characterized by varying degrees of severity, with five main subtypes classified by age of onset and symptom presentation [2]. These subtypes range from severe, early-onset forms like SMA type 0, to milder, later-onset forms like SMA type 4 [2][5]. The genetic basis of SMA lies in mutations of the *SMN1* gene, resulting in reduced production of a critical protein necessary for motor neuron survival [4]. Current research focuses on developing therapies to increase the amount of this protein in the body [6].

**Overview / Definition**

Spinal Muscular Atrophy (SMA) is a genetic disease affecting the central nervous system, peripheral nervous system, and voluntary muscle movement [1]. It is characterized by progressive muscle weakness and wasting [2][3]. The muscle weakness is typically more pronounced in muscles closer to the center of the body [2][3]. SMA arises from the degeneration and loss of motor neurons, which are nerve cells in the spinal cord that control muscle movement [2][4]. The absence of functional motor neurons disrupts the nerve signals required for muscle movement [2]. Importantly, SMA does not affect sensory nerves or intellect [4].

**Signs & Symptoms**

The symptoms of SMA vary depending on the type of SMA. Muscle weakness is a primary symptom in all types [2][3].

*   **SMA type 0 (congenital SMA):** Characterized by decreased fetal movements before birth, severe muscle weakness, and respiratory failure. Death typically occurs at birth or within the first month of life [2].
*   **SMA type 1 (infantile-onset SMA or Werdnig-Hoffman disease):** The most severe form, with onset within the first six months of life. Affected children may die before their second birthday [4].
*   **SMA type 2 (intermediate SMA):** Develops between six and 18 months. Children may be able to sit up but experience respiratory challenges that can shorten their lives [4].
*   **SMA type 3 (Kugelberg-Welander disease):** Symptoms appear after 18 months of age. Children can walk independently but may have difficulty [5].
*   **SMA type 4:** Develops after 18 years of age, with symptoms including mild to moderate leg muscle weakness [5].

**Genetics & Pathophysiology**

SMA is caused by mutations in the *SMN1* (survival motor neuron 1) gene [4]. Most individuals with SMA are missing a portion of the *SMN1* gene, leading to impaired SMN protein production [3]. The SMN protein is essential for the survival and function of motor neurons. Another gene, *SMN2*, also produces SMN protein, but at significantly lower levels compared to *SMN1* [6]. The number of copies of the *SMN2* gene can influence the severity of SMA [2]. Individuals with more copies of *SMN2* tend to have milder symptoms because they can produce slightly more SMN protein.

**Diagnosis**

The diagnosis of SMA typically involves genetic testing to identify mutations or deletions in the *SMN1* gene [3][4].

**Management / Treatment**

Currently, there is no cure for SMA [2][7]. However, treatments are available to manage symptoms and improve quality of life [2]. Treatment approaches often focus on increasing the amount of survival motor neuron protein in the body [6]. It is crucial to initiate therapy as soon as possible after diagnosis [6].

**Prognosis / Natural History**

The prognosis for individuals with SMA varies significantly depending on the type and severity of the condition [2]. SMA type 1 typically has the poorest prognosis, while SMA type 4 generally has the best [4][5].

**Epidemiology**

SMA is a relatively rare disease [5].

**History / Nomenclature**

SMA type 1 is also known as Werdnig-Hoffman disease [4]. SMA type 3 is also known as Kugelberg-Welander disease [5].

**Research & Trials**

Ongoing research efforts are focused on developing new therapies for SMA, including gene therapies and treatments that target the *SMN2* gene to increase SMN protein production [6].

**Uncertainties & Controversies**
