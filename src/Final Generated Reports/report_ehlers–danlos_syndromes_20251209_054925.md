## Ehlers-Danlos Syndromes

Ehlers-Danlos syndromes (EDS) are a group of thirteen individual genetic conditions that affect the body’s connective tissues [1, 2, 3]. Connective tissue plays a vital role in the body, lying between other tissues and organs, keeping them separate while connecting them, holding everything in place, and providing support [1]. In individuals with EDS, a gene mutation leads to the production of connective tissue that is fragile and stretchy [1].

EDS can manifest in various ways, impacting the joints, skin, and blood vessels [2]. Common symptoms across the different types of EDS include flexible and loose joints, also known as hypermobility, and fragile skin that bruises easily or heals poorly [2]. Furthermore, individuals with EDS may exhibit an increased range of joint movement [3] and skin that can be stretched beyond the normal range [4].

## Overview / Definition

Ehlers-Danlos syndromes (EDS) represent a heterogeneous group of heritable connective tissue disorders [1, 2, 3]. Connective tissue, which is affected in EDS, is the biological “glue” that provides structure and support to various parts of the body, including skin, joints, blood vessels, and internal organs [1]. The underlying cause of EDS is a gene mutation which results in the abnormal production or processing of collagen and other proteins that make up connective tissue, leading to fragile and stretchy tissues [1].

## Signs & Symptoms

The signs and symptoms of EDS vary depending on the specific type and the individual affected [2]. However, some common features are shared across many types. These include joint hypermobility, which can result in frequent dislocations and subluxations [5, 8], and skin abnormalities such as increased elasticity, fragility, and easy bruising [2, 4]. Individuals with EDS may experience an increased range of joint movement [3].

## Genetics & Pathophysiology

EDS is caused by genetic mutations that affect the structure, production, or processing of collagen and other connective tissue proteins [1]. These mutations lead to weakened connective tissue, resulting in the characteristic signs and symptoms of the syndromes [1]. The specific gene affected and the type of mutation determine the type of EDS and the severity of symptoms [1, 2, 3].

## Diagnosis

Healthcare providers classify EDS into 13 types based on where it affects the body and the symptoms experienced [5]. The 2017 international classification includes 13 types of EDS [6]. Each type has a descriptive name which is abbreviated using a lowercase letter to differentiate between the types [6]. The different types of EDS are no longer described using numbers or Roman numerals [6]. Diagnosis typically involves a physical examination, assessment of joint hypermobility using tools such as the Beighton score, and evaluation of skin and other affected tissues. Genetic testing can confirm the diagnosis in some types of EDS.

## Management / Treatment

Currently, there are no disease-specific treatments for any type of EDS [6]. Management focuses on alleviating symptoms and preventing complications [6]. A multidisciplinary approach is often necessary, involving various specialists such as physical therapists, pain management specialists, and surgeons [6].

## Prognosis / Natural History

The prognosis of EDS varies widely depending on the type and severity of the condition [2, 3]. Some individuals experience mild symptoms that can be managed with conservative treatment, while others may face significant disability and life-threatening complications [7]. Vascular EDS (vEDS), in particular, carries a risk of arterial dissections and ruptures, which can be life-threatening [7].

## Epidemiology

Information on the exact prevalence of EDS is limited, but it is estimated to affect at least 1 in 5,000 individuals [5]. Hypermobile EDS (hEDS) is believed to be the most common type [5, 8], although its exact prevalence is unknown.

## History / Nomenclature

The understanding of EDS has evolved over time, with the initial descriptions of the condition dating back to the late 19th and early 20th centuries [1]. The classification of EDS has also changed, with the most recent revision occurring in 2017, resulting in the current classification of 13 subtypes [6]. Each type has a descriptive name which is abbreviated using a lowercase letter to differentiate between the types [6].

## Research & Trials

Ongoing research is focused on improving the understanding of the genetic basis of EDS, developing more effective diagnostic tools, and identifying potential therapeutic targets [1]. Clinical trials are underway to evaluate the efficacy of various interventions, including medications, physical therapy, and surgical procedures.

## Uncertainties & Controversies

One of the main uncertainties in the field of EDS is the diagnosis of hypermobile EDS (hEDS) [5, 8]. The diagnostic criteria for hEDS are primarily clinical and lack specific genetic markers, making diagnosis challenging. This has led to controversies regarding the prevalence and diagnostic accuracy of hEDS.
