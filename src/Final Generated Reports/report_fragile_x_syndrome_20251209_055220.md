## Fragile X Syndrome

### Lead / Summary

Fragile X syndrome (FXS) is a genetic condition that represents the most common inherited cause of intellectual disability [1][2][3]. Individuals with FXS experience a range of developmental and behavioral challenges, varying in severity [3]. It is caused by a mutation in the _Fragile X Messenger Ribonucleoprotein 1_ (_FMR1_) gene on the X chromosome [3].

The condition affects both males and females, but symptoms are often milder in females [2]. Early diagnosis and intervention can significantly improve the quality of life for individuals with FXS [1]. While there is no cure for Fragile X syndrome, therapies and support can help individuals learn new skills and manage their symptoms [1][2].

### Overview / Definition

Fragile X syndrome (FXS) is a genetic disorder that affects brain development [2][3][4]. It is characterized by intellectual disability, behavioral and learning challenges, and distinct physical features [3][4]. FXS results from a mutation in the _FMR1_ gene, leading to a deficiency in the FMRP protein, which is essential for normal brain function [2]. Another name for Fragile X syndrome is Martin-Bell syndrome [1].

### Signs & Symptoms

The signs and symptoms of Fragile X syndrome vary but commonly include intellectual problems, physical features, behavioral challenges, speech and language problems, and sensory issues [3]. Some physical characteristics include a narrow face, large head, large ears, flexible joints, flat feet, and a prominent forehead [3][4]. Behavioral symptoms include anxiety, hyperactive behavior, fidgeting, impulsive actions, and ADHD [1]. People with Fragile X syndrome may also have trouble with social situations and may be sensitive to loud sounds, crowds, or bright lights [1].

### Genetics & Pathophysiology

Fragile X syndrome is caused by changes in the _FMR1_ gene located on the X chromosome [3]. This gene contains a segment of DNA, known as a CGG repeat, that is repeated multiple times [2]. In individuals without FXS, this CGG repeat is repeated a limited number of times. However, in people with FXS, the CGG repeat is expanded, leading to a "full mutation" [2]. This expanded repeat silences the _FMR1_ gene, preventing it from producing the FMRP protein [2]. The FMRP protein plays a critical role in the development and function of synapses, which are connections between nerve cells in the brain [2]. A deficiency in FMRP leads to the intellectual disability and other symptoms associated with FXS [2].

### Diagnosis

Fragile X syndrome is typically diagnosed through a blood test that analyzes the _FMR1_ gene [1]. This test determines the number of CGG repeats in the _FMR1_ gene [2]. A full mutation, indicating FXS, is identified by a significantly expanded number of CGG repeats [2]. Genetic testing can also identify individuals with a "premutation," which is a smaller expansion of the CGG repeat that does not cause FXS but can lead to other Fragile X-associated disorders [2].

### Management / Treatment

There is no cure for Fragile X syndrome [1][2]. However, a range of treatments and interventions are available to help manage the symptoms and improve the quality of life for individuals with FXS [1]. These treatments often include speech therapy, special education, and behavioral support [1]. Medications can also be used to manage specific symptoms, such as anxiety, hyperactivity, and seizures [2].

### Prognosis / Natural History

The lifespan for people with FXS is usually unaffected because there are typically no life-threatening health concerns associated with the condition [1]. Early intervention and ongoing support can help people with Fragile X syndrome learn new skills and lead fulfilling lives [1]. The severity of symptoms varies among individuals, with some experiencing more significant challenges than others [3].

### Epidemiology

Fragile X syndrome affects both males and females, but males are generally more severely affected [2]. Worldwide, it's estimated that about 1 in 7,000 males and 1 in 11,000 females have FXS [1][2].

### History / Nomenclature

Fragile X syndrome was first described in 1943 by Martin and Bell, who observed X-linked intellectual disability in a family [1]. The term "Fragile X" refers to the characteristic "fragile" appearance of the X chromosome under a microscope in affected individuals [1].

### Research & Trials

Ongoing research efforts are focused on developing new treatments for Fragile X syndrome [3][4]. These studies include investigations into potential medications that could improve brain function and reduce behavioral problems in individuals with FXS [4]. Research is also exploring the underlying mechanisms of FXS to identify novel therapeutic targets [4].

### Uncertainties & Controversies

One area of uncertainty in Fragile X syndrome relates to the variability of symptoms, particularly in females [2]. The exact reasons for this variability are not fully understood [2]. There is also ongoing debate regarding the most effective treatment strategies for managing specific symptoms, such as anxiety and hyperactivity [1].
