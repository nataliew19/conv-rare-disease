## Primary Sclerosing Cholangitis

Primary Sclerosing Cholangitis (PSC) is a chronic liver disease characterized by inflammation and scarring of the bile ducts, both inside and outside the liver [1][2]. This process leads to the narrowing or blockage of these ducts, causing bile to accumulate within the liver and resulting in further liver damage [1]. It's important to recognize that PSC is a rare condition, affecting an estimated 5 to 16 out of every 100,000 individuals [1][2].

Over time, the ongoing damage to the liver can lead to cirrhosis and, ultimately, liver failure [1][2]. While there is currently no cure for PSC, treatments are available to manage symptoms and complications, and liver transplantation remains a curative option for severe cases [3][6][2]. Research continues to explore the underlying causes and potential therapies for this challenging condition [1][5].

### Overview / Definition

Primary Sclerosing Cholangitis (PSC) is a chronic disease that affects the bile ducts in the liver [1][2]. Bile ducts are like a network of pipes that carry bile, a fluid produced by the liver that helps with digestion, to the small intestine [1]. In PSC, these bile ducts become inflamed and scarred, causing them to narrow or become blocked [1][2]. This narrowing slows or stops the flow of bile, causing it to back up into the liver. The build-up of bile leads to further inflammation and damage to the liver cells [1]. Over time, this can result in cirrhosis, which is scarring of the liver, and eventually liver failure [1].

### Signs & Symptoms

Many people with PSC may not experience any symptoms, especially in the early stages of the disease [3][4][2]. In fact, PSC is often discovered during testing for other conditions [3][6]. When symptoms do appear, they can vary from person to person [4][2]. Common symptoms include fatigue, itching (pruritus), abdominal discomfort, and jaundice, a yellowing of the skin and eyes [4][2]. As the disease progresses, more severe symptoms can develop, such as abdominal pain, fever, and chills, which may indicate a bile duct infection [1][3][2]. Complications of cirrhosis, such as ascites (fluid build-up in the abdomen) and hepatic encephalopathy (confusion due to liver dysfunction), can also occur [1][3][2].

### Genetics & Pathophysiology

The exact cause of Primary Sclerosing Cholangitis remains unknown [1][5][2]. However, researchers believe that a combination of factors contributes to the development of the disease [1][5]. These factors may include autoimmune responses, where the body's immune system mistakenly attacks the bile ducts; genetic predisposition, suggesting that some people may be more likely to develop PSC due to their genes; and environmental triggers, such as infections or exposure to certain toxins [1][5]. It is also noted that having a family member with PSC, the presence of certain bacteria and viruses, co-existing inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), and underlying immune system problems may also contribute [5].

### Diagnosis

Primary Sclerosing Cholangitis is often diagnosed incidentally during routine blood tests or imaging performed for other reasons [3][6]. Doctors use a combination of tests to diagnose PSC. Blood tests are used to measure liver enzyme levels, which are often elevated in people with PSC [3][6]. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP), a non-invasive imaging technique, is used to visualize the bile ducts and look for the characteristic narrowing and beading pattern associated with PSC [3][6]. MRCP uses magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) to create detailed pictures of the bile ducts [3][6].

### Management / Treatment

Currently, there is no cure for Primary Sclerosing Cholangitis [3][6][2]. Therefore, treatment focuses on managing the symptoms, slowing the progression of liver damage, and preventing or treating complications [3][6][2]. Medications can be prescribed to relieve itching and address any vitamin deficiencies [6]. Ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) may also be prescribed to help improve bile flow. For individuals with severe PSC, a liver transplant is currently the only treatment option that can cure the condition [3][6][2].

### Prognosis / Natural History

Primary Sclerosing Cholangitis is a slowly progressive disease, and the rate of progression varies significantly among individuals [4][2]. Some people may live for many years with few or no symptoms, while others may experience a more rapid decline in liver function [4][2]. Liver failure may occur 10-15 years after diagnosis, but this may take even longer for some PSC patients [5].

### Epidemiology

Primary Sclerosing Cholangitis is considered a rare disease, affecting an estimated 5 to 16 out of every 100,000 people [1][2]. It is more commonly diagnosed in individuals between the ages of 30 and 40, although it can occur at any age [1][5]. Men are affected approximately twice as often as women [1]. There is a strong association between PSC and inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), particularly ulcerative colitis [1][5][2]. A significant proportion of individuals with PSC also have IBD [1][5][2].

### Research & Trials

Researchers are actively working to better understand the causes of Primary Sclerosing Cholangitis, identify new treatments, and improve the lives of people living with the disease [1][5]. Research efforts are focused on several key areas, including identifying genetic factors that may predispose individuals to PSC, investigating the role of the immune system in the development of the disease, and developing new therapies to target the underlying causes of PSC [1][2][5]. Patients may be eligible for clinical trials that are testing new medications and other treatments for PSC.

### Uncertainties & Controversies

Despite ongoing research, the exact cause of PSC remains a mystery [1][5][2]. While associations with autoimmune responses, genetics, and environmental triggers have been identified, the specific mechanisms by which these factors contribute to the disease are not fully understood [1][5]. The optimal treatment strategy for PSC is also a subject of ongoing debate. While liver transplantation is a curative option, it is reserved for severe cases [3][6][2]. The use of medications, such as ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA), remains controversial, with conflicting evidence regarding its long-term effectiveness [6].
