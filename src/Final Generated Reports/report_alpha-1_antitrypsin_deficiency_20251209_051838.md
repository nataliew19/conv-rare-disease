## Alpha-1 Antitrypsin Deficiency

**Lead / Summary**

Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency (AATD), often referred to as Alpha-1, is a genetic condition that can lead to lung and/or liver damage [1][2]. This inherited disorder is characterized by insufficient levels of alpha-1 antitrypsin (A1AT), a crucial protein found in the bloodstream [3]. The deficiency elevates the risk of developing lung diseases such as emphysema, and liver conditions like cirrhosis [2][4]. AATD is also sometimes referred to as genetic COPD or genetic emphysema [1].

The root cause of AATD lies in inherited genetic mutations [2]. Specifically, changes in the SERPINA1 gene, responsible for providing instructions for A1AT protein production, are to blame [2][5]. Individuals inheriting two mutated copies of this gene are predisposed to AATD, significantly raising their risk of experiencing lung disease or liver damage before the age of 45 [2]. While there is no cure, early diagnosis and proper management can significantly improve the quality of life for individuals with AATD [1][6].

**Overview / Definition**

Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency (AATD) is an inherited condition characterized by low levels of alpha-1 antitrypsin (A1AT) in the blood [2][3]. A1AT is a protein primarily produced in the liver and released into the bloodstream, where it functions to protect the lungs from damage [3]. Specifically, A1AT inhibits enzymes like elastase, which can break down lung tissue. Think of A1AT as a bodyguard for the lungs [3]. In AATD, the reduced levels of A1AT can lead to both lung and liver problems [2][4].

**Signs & Symptoms**

Symptoms of AATD can manifest early in life, although many individuals may not experience any symptoms until middle age [6]. Lung-related symptoms include a chronic cough, often accompanied by sputum production, and frequent lung infections [6]. The associated lung disease is marked by progressive degenerative changes that destroy the lungs, known as emphysema [3]. Emphysema symptoms include shortness of breath, chronic cough, phlegm production, and wheezing [3]. Liver-related symptoms can include jaundice, swelling of the abdomen, and poor feeding, particularly in infants [7]. It is important to note that some individuals with severe A1AT deficiency may never develop emphysema, especially if they have never smoked [3].

**Genetics & Pathophysiology**

AATD is caused by mutations in the SERPINA1 gene, which provides the body with instructions for making the A1AT protein [2][5]. Individuals inherit two copies of the SERPINA1 gene, one from each parent [2]. AATD typically occurs when an individual inherits two mutated copies of the SERPINA1 gene [2]. The lack of functional A1AT allows enzymes to damage lung tissue, leading to emphysema [3]. The deficiency can also cause liver damage, potentially due to the buildup of abnormal A1AT protein within liver cells [7].

**Diagnosis**

Diagnosing AATD can be challenging because there is no single definitive symptom [5]. The diagnostic process involves a clinical examination of the respiratory system and chest, followed by blood tests to measure AAT levels [5]. Genetic tests are also performed to check for mutations in the SERPINA1 gene [5].

**Management / Treatment**

While there is no cure for AATD, treatment focuses on managing symptoms and preventing further damage [1]. Early diagnosis and treatment are crucial for better outcomes [6]. Treatment options include augmentation therapy, which aims to increase AAT levels in the blood [6][8]. Bronchodilators and inhaled corticosteroids can help control COPD symptoms [6][8]. Pulmonary rehabilitation can improve lung function and quality of life. In severe cases, lung transplantation may be considered [6][8]. Lifestyle modifications, such as avoiding smoking, environmental dusts, workplace exposure to toxic substances, and minimizing alcohol consumption, are also essential [6][8].

**Prognosis / Natural History**

The prognosis for individuals with AATD varies depending on the severity of the deficiency, the presence of lung or liver disease, and lifestyle factors such as smoking [3]. Individuals diagnosed before pulmonary symptoms occur typically have better outcomes [6]. However, even with treatment, the disease can progress, leading to significant morbidity and mortality.

**Epidemiology**

AATD is an inherited condition, and individuals with a family history of AATD, emphysema (especially at a young age), or liver disease are at higher risk [2][6]. People of Northern European descent are at a higher risk of having AATD [7].

**History / Nomenclature**

AATD was first described in 1963 by Carl-Bertil Laurell and Sten Eriksson in Sweden. The condition is also referred to as genetic COPD or genetic emphysema [1].

**Research & Trials**

Ongoing research efforts are focused on developing new therapies for AATD, including gene therapy and other novel treatments. **Uncertainties & Controversies**

Despite the available treatments, uncertainties remain regarding the optimal management of AATD. One area of debate is the initiation and duration of augmentation therapy, particularly in individuals with mild to moderate AAT deficiency. Some studies suggest that augmentation therapy can slow the progression of lung disease, while others have not shown a significant benefit. The role of liver transplantation in AATD-related liver disease is also an area of ongoing research and discussion.
