## Primary Biliary Cholangitis

### Lead / Summary

Primary biliary cholangitis (PBC) is a chronic liver disease characterized by progressive destruction of the small bile ducts within the liver [1][2][3]. This autoimmune condition leads to impaired bile flow, causing bile to accumulate in the liver, resulting in inflammation, scarring (fibrosis), and potentially cirrhosis [1][2][3]. While there is currently no cure for PBC, early diagnosis and treatment can help manage symptoms and slow disease progression, allowing many individuals to live for years with a good quality of life [1].

The condition is more prevalent in women and typically manifests during middle age, between 45 and 65 years old [1][3][4]. Diagnosis involves blood tests to detect specific antibodies and assess liver function, and in some cases, a liver biopsy [1]. Treatment primarily focuses on medications like ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) to improve bile flow and manage symptoms such as itching [1]. In advanced cases of PBC, a liver transplant may become necessary [1].

### Overview / Definition

Primary biliary cholangitis (PBC) is a chronic, progressive autoimmune disease of the liver [1][2][3]. The hallmark of PBC is the gradual destruction of the small bile ducts inside the liver [1][2][3]. These bile ducts are responsible for transporting bile, a fluid that aids in digestion and waste removal, away from the liver. The resulting impairment of bile flow leads to a buildup of bile within the liver itself, initiating a cascade of inflammation and damage [1].

### Signs & Symptoms

In the early stages of PBC, many individuals experience no noticeable symptoms [1]. As the disease progresses, a variety of symptoms may emerge. Common symptoms include persistent fatigue, itching (pruritus), and signs of biliary disease [1][4]. Some individuals may also experience pain or discomfort in the upper right portion of their abdomen [2]. Women with PBC may also report experiencing frequent yeast infections [1].

### Genetics & Pathophysiology

The exact cause of PBC remains unknown, but it is considered an autoimmune disorder [1]. This means that the body's immune system mistakenly attacks its own tissues, in this case, the small bile ducts of the liver. The resulting inflammation and damage to these ducts obstructs the normal flow of bile [1]. The buildup of bile in the liver leads to further inflammation and scarring (fibrosis) [1][2][3]. Over time, this can progress to cirrhosis, where healthy liver tissue is replaced by scar tissue, impairing liver function [1].

### Diagnosis

Diagnosing PBC involves a comprehensive approach that includes a review of the patient's medical history, a physical examination, and specific diagnostic tests [1]. Blood tests play a crucial role in identifying antibodies associated with PBC, such as the antimitochondrial antibody (AMA) [1]. Blood tests can also reveal indicators of liver stress or damage [1]. Imaging techniques, such as ultrasonography, are often used to visualize the liver and biliary system and to rule out other potential causes of liver disease [1]. In some instances, a liver biopsy, where a small sample of liver tissue is examined under a microscope, may be necessary to confirm the diagnosis [1].

### Management / Treatment

While there is currently no cure for PBC, treatment focuses on managing the disease and slowing down the progression of liver damage [1][4]. Ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) is a commonly prescribed medication that helps improve bile flow from the liver and reduce liver damage [1]. Additional treatments are aimed at managing individual symptoms, such as itching, and preventing complications like vitamin deficiencies [1].

### Prognosis / Natural History

The prognosis for individuals with PBC can vary. With early diagnosis and appropriate treatment, many people can effectively manage their symptoms and live for many years without significant disruption to their daily lives [1]. However, without treatment, the disease can progress to liver failure, requiring a liver transplant [3].

### Epidemiology

PBC is more prevalent in women than in men [1][3]. In the United States, it is estimated to affect approximately 60 females per 100,000 and 15 males per 100,000 [1]. The disorder typically becomes apparent during middle age, often affecting individuals between 45 and 65 years old [4]. It is also more common among white individuals and those with a family history of autoimmune diseases [1].

### History / Nomenclature

PBC was previously known as primary biliary cirrhosis. The name was changed to primary biliary cholangitis to better reflect the fact that many individuals with the disease do not have cirrhosis, particularly in the early stages [1]. The term "cholangitis" refers to inflammation of the bile ducts, which is a key feature of the disease [1][2][3].

### Research & Trials

Ongoing research efforts are focused on better understanding the causes of PBC, developing new and more effective treatments, and improving the overall management of the disease. Clinical trials are underway to evaluate the safety and efficacy of novel therapies for PBC.

### Uncertainties & Controversies

While significant progress has been made in understanding and managing PBC, several uncertainties and controversies remain. The exact environmental triggers that may contribute to the development of PBC in genetically susceptible individuals are still being investigated. The optimal management strategies for individuals who do not respond adequately to UDCA remain a topic of ongoing research and debate. Additionally, the long-term risks and benefits of emerging therapies are still being evaluated.
