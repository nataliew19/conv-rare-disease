## Neurofibromatosis Type 1

Neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1) is a genetic disorder characterized by the growth of tumors along nerves and on the skin [1, 2]. It is a multisystem condition that can affect various parts of the body [1]. Formerly known as Von Recklinghausen disease, NF1 affects both males and females equally, with no preference for race or ethnicity [1, 3, 4]. The symptoms and severity of NF1 can vary significantly among affected individuals [2].

NF1 is a progressive disease, meaning it can worsen over time [3, 5]. While there is no cure for NF1, treatments are available to manage the symptoms and improve the quality of life for individuals with this condition [6]. These treatments can include surgery, medications for tumor growth, and medications for other associated symptoms [3].

## Overview / Definition

Neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1) is a genetic disorder that primarily affects the development and growth of nerve cells [1, 2]. This can lead to the formation of tumors called neurofibromas, which can grow on nerves throughout the body, including the skin [3]. NF1 is considered a multisystem disorder because it can affect various parts of the body, including the skin, eyes, and nervous system [1]. The severity of NF1 can vary greatly, with some individuals experiencing mild symptoms while others have more significant complications [2].

## Signs & Symptoms

The signs and symptoms of NF1 can vary widely from person to person [2]. Some individuals may have mild symptoms that are not immediately apparent, while others may experience more pronounced and noticeable features [2]. Common signs and symptoms of NF1 include the presence of multiple café au lait spots, which are flat, pigmented birthmarks on the skin [3]. Neurofibromas, which are tumors that grow under the skin, are another characteristic feature [3]. Freckling in areas such as the armpits, groin, or under the breasts is also commonly observed [3]. Most people with NF1 have recognizable symptoms often affecting the skin, eyes and nervous system, commonly before age 10 [4]. Other potential symptoms include Lisch nodules (small, benign growths on the iris of the eye), tumors that grow along the optic nerve, skeletal abnormalities, and learning challenges [7, 8, 9].

## Genetics & Pathophysiology

NF1 is caused by a genetic change in the NF1 gene, which is responsible for producing the neurofibromin protein [3]. This protein plays a crucial role in regulating cell growth and division. A mutation in the NF1 gene can lead to a deficiency in neurofibromin, resulting in uncontrolled cell growth and the formation of tumors [3]. The genetic change can be inherited from a parent who has NF1, or it can occur spontaneously in a new individual [6, 3].

## Management / Treatment

While there is currently no cure for NF1, various treatments are available to help manage the symptoms and improve the quality of life for individuals with the condition [6]. Treatment approaches are tailored to the specific symptoms and needs of each individual [3]. Surgery may be performed to remove or reduce the size of tumors [3]. Medications may be prescribed to address tumor growth or manage other associated symptoms, such as ADHD [3]. Recent advances have led to the approval of selumetinib for pediatric patients with inoperable plexiform neurofibromas and mirdametinib for adult and pediatric patients with plexiform neurofibromas that cannot be completely removed by surgery [7].

## Epidemiology

The incidence of NF1 is approximately 1 in 3500 live births [5]. This makes it one of the most common genetic disorders affecting the nervous system. NF1 affects males and females equally and does not show a preference for race or ethnicity [3, 4].

## History / Nomenclature

Neurofibromatosis type 1 was formerly known as Von Recklinghausen disease, named after the German physician Friedrich Daniel von Recklinghausen, who first described the condition in the late 19th century [1]. The term "neurofibromatosis" refers to the growth of tumors on nerves, while "type 1" distinguishes it from other forms of neurofibromatosis, such as NF2 and schwannomatosis [1].

## Uncertainties & Controversies

The variability in the presentation of NF1 poses diagnostic challenges, particularly in individuals with mild symptoms [2]. The optimal management strategies for certain complications, such as learning disabilities and skeletal abnormalities, remain areas of ongoing research and debate. The long-term prognosis for individuals with NF1 can be difficult to predict, as the course of the disease can vary widely [2].
