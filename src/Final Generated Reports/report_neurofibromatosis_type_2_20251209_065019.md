**Neurofibromatosis Type 2**

**Lead / Summary**

Neurofibromatosis type 2 (NF2) is a genetic disorder that primarily affects the nervous system, leading to the development of tumors on nerves in the brain and spine [1], [2]. These tumors, while typically benign, can cause a variety of symptoms depending on their location and size. NF2 is also known as NF2-related schwannomatosis [1]. Management focuses on monitoring tumor growth and alleviating symptoms, as there is currently no cure [1]. A multidisciplinary team of specialists is crucial in providing comprehensive care for individuals with NF2 [4].

The hallmark of NF2 is the presence of bilateral vestibular schwannomas, tumors on the nerves responsible for hearing and balance [2], [3]. These tumors often lead to hearing loss, tinnitus, and balance problems. Other types of tumors, such as meningiomas and ependymomas, can also develop in the brain and spinal cord [2], [3]. Although NF2 is a genetic condition, about half of cases arise from a new mutation, meaning the affected individual has no family history of the disorder [3]. Early diagnosis and intervention are essential for managing the disease effectively and improving the quality of life for those affected.

**Overview / Definition**

Neurofibromatosis type 2 (NF2) is a genetic disorder characterized by the growth of multiple tumors in the central nervous system (CNS) [2]. The condition is also referred to as NF2-related schwannomatosis [1]. The estimated incidence of NF2 is approximately 1 in 37,000 births per year [2]. NF2 results from a mutation in the *NF2* gene located on chromosome 22. This gene normally functions as a tumor suppressor, preventing uncontrolled cell growth [3].

**Signs & Symptoms**

Symptoms of NF2 can vary widely among affected individuals, with the typical age of onset being in the late teens or early 20s, although some individuals may not experience symptoms until later in life [3]. Common initial signs include hearing loss, ringing in the ears (tinnitus), and balance problems [3], [2]. Eye problems, particularly juvenile cataracts, can sometimes be an early indication of NF2, especially in childhood [3].

**Genetics & Pathophysiology**

NF2 is caused by a mutation in the *NF2* gene located on chromosome 22 [3]. This gene produces a protein called merlin (or schwannomin), which acts as a tumor suppressor. The *NF2* gene normally prevents cells from growing and dividing too quickly [3]. Mutations in the *NF2* gene disrupt the production of functional merlin, leading to uncontrolled cell growth and the formation of tumors, particularly schwannomas, meningiomas, and ependymomas [3], [2].

**Diagnosis**

Diagnosis of NF2 is typically based on a combination of clinical findings, imaging studies, and genetic testing. Diagnostic criteria often include the presence of bilateral vestibular schwannomas [3]. Other criteria may involve a unilateral vestibular schwannoma in combination with a family history of NF2 or the presence of other NF2-related tumors, such as meningiomas, gliomas, or schwannomas [3]. Physical and neurological examinations are important for assessing the extent of neurological involvement. Hearing tests (audiological evaluation) are conducted to assess hearing loss and auditory function [3]. MRI scans are crucial for detecting tumors in the brain and spinal cord [3]. Genetic testing can confirm the diagnosis by identifying mutations in the *NF2* gene [3].

**Management / Treatment**

Currently, there is no cure for NF2, and treatment focuses on managing symptoms and controlling tumor growth [1]. A multidisciplinary approach is essential, involving specialists such as neurosurgeons, audiologists, ophthalmologists, and geneticists [4]. Surgical removal of tumors may be necessary to alleviate symptoms or prevent neurological complications [4]. Stereotactic radiosurgery can be used as an alternative to traditional surgery to control tumor growth [4]. Medical treatments, such as bevacizumab, may be used to reduce tumor size or prolong stability [4]. Other therapies, including physical therapy and occupational therapy, can help manage symptoms and improve quality of life [4].

**Prognosis / Natural History**

The prognosis for individuals with NF2 varies depending on the severity of the disease, the location and growth rate of tumors, and the individual's response to treatment [3]. The course of the disease can be highly variable, with some individuals experiencing relatively stable disease for many years, while others experience more rapid tumor growth and neurological decline [3]. Regular monitoring and follow-up appointments with specialists are essential for tracking tumor growth and managing symptoms effectively [3].

**Epidemiology**

The estimated incidence of NF2 is approximately 1 in 37,000 live births per year [2]. NF2 affects individuals of all races and ethnicities equally. Approximately half of NF2 cases are caused by new mutations in the *NF2* gene, meaning the affected individual has no family history of the condition [3]. The other half of cases are inherited from a parent with NF2, following an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern [3].

**History / Nomenclature**

NF2 was first recognized as a distinct clinical entity separate from neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1) in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. Initially, NF2 was often referred to as "central neurofibromatosis" due to the predominance of tumors affecting the central nervous system. The identification of the *NF2* gene on chromosome 22 in 1993 provided a molecular basis for the disorder and facilitated more accurate diagnosis and genetic counseling. The term "NF2-related schwannomatosis" is sometimes used to describe individuals with NF2 who primarily develop schwannomas but may not meet the strict diagnostic criteria for classical NF2 [1].

**Research & Trials**

Ongoing research efforts are focused on developing new and improved treatments for NF2 [4]. Clinical trials are investigating the effectiveness of various therapies, including targeted drug therapies, gene therapy, and immunotherapies. Researchers are also working to better understand the underlying mechanisms of tumor development in NF2 and to identify potential biomarkers for early diagnosis and prognosis [4].

**Uncertainties & Controversies**

Several uncertainties and controversies exist regarding the management of NF2. The optimal timing and approach to surgical resection of vestibular schwannomas remain a topic of debate, with some advocating for early intervention to preserve hearing, while others prefer a more conservative approach to minimize the risk of neurological complications. The role of medical therapies, such as bevacizumab, in the long-term management of NF2 is still being investigated, and the optimal duration and dosing of these agents are not yet fully established. The development of more effective and targeted therapies for NF2 remains a major area of unmet need. The understanding of mosaicism and its impact on the phenotypic expression of NF2 requires further research.
