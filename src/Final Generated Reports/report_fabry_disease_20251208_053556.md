## Fabry Disease

### Lead / Summary

Fabry disease is a rare, inherited genetic disorder caused by a deficiency in the enzyme alpha-galactosidase A [1]. This enzyme deficiency leads to the accumulation of a fatty substance called globotriaosylceramide (Gb3) within the body's cells [1]. As a consequence of Gb3 build-up, affected individuals can experience a wide range of symptoms, affecting multiple organ systems [3]. Fabry disease is classified as both a lysosomal storage disorder and a metabolic disorder due to its impact on cellular waste processing and biochemical pathways [2].

The disorder is X-linked, meaning the gene responsible for producing alpha-galactosidase A is located on the X chromosome [2, 4]. This inheritance pattern influences how the disease manifests in males and females, with males typically experiencing more severe symptoms [4]. While there is currently no cure for Fabry disease, treatments are available to manage symptoms and slow disease progression [7, 8]. These treatments include enzyme replacement therapy and oral chaperone therapy [6, 8].

### Overview / Definition

Fabry disease is a rare genetic condition stemming from a deficiency or malfunction of the enzyme alpha-galactosidase A (α-GAL A) [3]. This enzyme is essential for breaking down a specific type of fat, globotriaosylceramide (Gb3), within cells [1]. When α-GAL A is deficient or non-functional, Gb3 accumulates in the lysosomes of cells throughout the body, leading to a variety of signs and symptoms [1, 3]. Fabry disease is categorized as a lysosomal storage disorder because the enzyme deficiency disrupts the normal function of lysosomes, which are cellular organelles responsible for waste processing [2]. It is also classified as a metabolic disorder because the enzymatic defect interferes with the body's normal metabolic processes [2]. The *GLA* gene, which provides instructions for making the α-GAL A enzyme, is located on the X chromosome [2, 4].

### Signs & Symptoms

The signs and symptoms of Fabry disease are highly variable and can affect multiple organ systems [3]. One of the hallmark symptoms is acroparesthesia, characterized by pain and burning sensations in the hands and feet [1, 3]. Other common symptoms include angiokeratomas, which are small, dark red spots on the skin [1, 3], and hypohidrosis, a decreased ability to sweat [1]. Ocular manifestations can include corneal opacity or corneal verticillata, which refers to cloudiness or streaks in the cornea [1]. Gastrointestinal problems such as abdominal pain, diarrhea, and nausea are also frequently reported [1].

Additionally, individuals with Fabry disease may experience tinnitus (ringing in the ears) and hearing loss [1, 5]. Kidney dysfunction and eventual kidney failure are serious complications that can occur [3]. Cardiac abnormalities, such as heart enlargement and valve problems, are also frequently observed [3]. Neurological complications can arise as well [3]. Psychiatric manifestations such as depression and anxiety can also occur in individuals with Fabry disease [5]. Fabry crises, characterized by episodes of intense, burning pain, often starting in the hands and feet and spreading to other parts of the body, can also occur [5].

### Genetics & Pathophysiology

Fabry disease is caused by mutations in the *GLA* gene, which provides the instructions for producing the alpha-galactosidase A enzyme [3]. This enzyme is crucial for breaking down globotriaosylceramide (Gb3), a fatty substance that accumulates in lysosomes [1, 3]. When the *GLA* gene is mutated, it leads to a deficiency or dysfunction of α-GAL A [3]. As a result, Gb3 accumulates within the lysosomes of cells throughout the body, particularly affecting cells in the kidneys, heart, and nervous system [1, 3]. The progressive accumulation of Gb3 disrupts normal cell function, leading to the diverse range of symptoms associated with Fabry disease [1].

Since the *GLA* gene is located on the X chromosome, Fabry disease follows an X-linked inheritance pattern [2, 4]. Males, who have only one X chromosome, typically experience more severe symptoms because they lack a second, normal copy of the gene [4]. Females, who have two X chromosomes, may have a more variable presentation; some are asymptomatic, while others can be as severely affected as males [4]. This variability in females is due to random X-chromosome inactivation, where one of the two X chromosomes is randomly inactivated in each cell [4].

### Management / Treatment

Currently, there is no cure for Fabry disease [7]. The primary goals of treatment are to alleviate symptoms, slow disease progression, and prevent complications [8]. Management of Fabry disease typically requires a multidisciplinary approach involving specialists from various fields [9]. Treatment options include enzyme replacement therapy (ERT), oral chaperone therapy, conventional medical treatment, adjunct therapies, lifestyle modifications, and prophylactic medications [6, 8, 9].

Enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) is a primary treatment option that involves regular infusions of synthetic α-GAL A to replace the deficient enzyme [6, 8]. This helps to reduce the accumulation of Gb3 in cells [8]. Oral chaperone therapy uses small molecules to bind to and stabilize faulty alpha-GAL enzymes, helping them to function properly [8]. Conventional medical treatments are used to manage specific symptoms, such as pain medications for acroparesthesia and medications to control blood pressure and kidney function [9]. Lifestyle modifications, such as avoiding strenuous exercise and extreme temperatures, can also help manage symptoms [9].

### Epidemiology

Fabry disease is estimated to affect approximately 1 in 1,000 to 1 in 9,000 individuals [1]. The prevalence may vary in different populations [1]. Males with classic and later-onset phenotypes typically experience more severe symptoms than affected females [4]. Females can be asymptomatic or as severely affected as their male relatives [4].

### Uncertainties & Controversies

One uncertainty in Fabry disease management is the optimal timing to initiate enzyme replacement therapy (ERT). There is ongoing debate about whether to start ERT in asymptomatic individuals or to wait until symptoms develop. Another area of controversy revolves around the effectiveness of ERT in reversing established organ damage, particularly in the heart and kidneys. Some studies have shown benefits, while others have reported limited efficacy. The variable presentation of Fabry disease in females also contributes to diagnostic challenges, as symptoms may be subtle or non-specific, leading to delayed or missed diagnoses [4].
