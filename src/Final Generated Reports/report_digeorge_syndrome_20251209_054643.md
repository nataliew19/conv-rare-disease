## DiGeorge Syndrome: A Comprehensive Overview

DiGeorge syndrome (DGS), also known as 22q11.2 deletion syndrome (22qDS), is a congenital disorder arising from a microdeletion on chromosome 22 [1]. This genetic anomaly disrupts the proper development of structures derived from the pharyngeal pouches, influencing the formation of the middle and external ear, maxilla, mandible, palatine tonsils, thyroid, parathyroids, thymus, aortic arch, and cardiac outflow tract [1]. The syndrome presents a wide spectrum of medical issues, with the severity differing significantly among affected individuals [2].

Individuals with DGS can exhibit a variety of features, including cardiac anomalies, recurrent infections, abnormal facial features, thymic hypoplasia or aplasia, cleft palate, developmental delay, and hypocalcemia [1]. Additional challenges may involve learning and behavioral difficulties, such as ADHD, along with speech and hearing impairments, and difficulties with feeding [3][2]. Diagnosis typically occurs shortly after birth through genetic testing designed to identify the 22q11.2 deletion [3]. Treatment strategies are tailored to address specific health concerns as they arise and often necessitate a collaborative approach involving multiple medical specialists [4][5].

### Overview / Definition

DiGeorge syndrome (DGS) is a congenital disorder characterized by the deletion of a small segment of chromosome 22 at the 22q11.2 locus [1]. This genetic deletion disrupts normal development, particularly affecting structures derived from the pharyngeal pouches [1]. Consequently, the development of various systems including the cardiac, immune, and endocrine systems can be compromised [1].

### Signs & Symptoms

The signs and symptoms of DiGeorge syndrome are highly variable, even among individuals with the same genetic deletion [2]. Common features include heart defects, such as tetralogy of Fallot and interrupted aortic arch [1]. Immune deficiency, stemming from thymic hypoplasia or aplasia, leads to recurrent infections [1]. Other features can include abnormal facial features (e.g., small jaw, wide-set eyes), cleft palate, developmental delay, and hypocalcemia due to hypoparathyroidism [1]. Additional issues such as learning and behavior problems (ADHD), speech and hearing problems, mouth and feeding difficulties, kidney problems, and hormone problems can also occur [2, 3]. The range of severity varies considerably [2].

### Genetics & Pathophysiology

DiGeorge syndrome is caused by a microdeletion on the long arm of chromosome 22, specifically at the 22q11.2 region [1]. This deletion typically occurs randomly during the formation of egg or sperm cells, or early in fetal development, and is usually not inherited, although it can be in some instances [3]. The deleted region contains multiple genes, including *TBX1*, which plays a crucial role in the development of the pharyngeal arches and related structures [1]. The loss of these genes disrupts normal development, leading to the characteristic features of DiGeorge syndrome [1].

### Diagnosis

Diagnosis of DiGeorge syndrome often occurs soon after birth through a blood test (genetic testing) to identify the 22q11.2 deletion [3]. This test, typically fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) or chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA), can confirm the presence of the deletion [3]. A genetic consult is essential, alongside a thorough assessment of the clinical presentation [4]. In cases of complete DiGeorge syndrome, characterized by severe immune deficiency, flow cytometry can be used to assess the presence and function of T cells [2].

### Management / Treatment

There is no cure for DiGeorge syndrome; treatment focuses on managing the various health issues as they arise [5]. Management requires a multidisciplinary approach involving a team of medical specialists, including cardiologists, immunologists, endocrinologists, and developmental pediatricians [4, 5]. Specific management strategies include speech therapy, physical and occupational therapy, surgery to repair organ defects (e.g., heart defects, cleft palate), and special education programs [5]. Hypocalcemia is treated with calcium and vitamin D supplementation [1]. Immune deficiency may require prophylactic antibiotics or, in severe cases, hematopoietic stem cell transplantation [4].

### Prognosis / Natural History

The severity of DiGeorge syndrome varies significantly, impacting the prognosis [3, 5]. Some individuals experience life-threatening complications, particularly related to heart problems or severe immune deficiency [3, 5]. However, with ongoing treatment and support, many adults with DiGeorge syndrome can lead active and fulfilling lives [5]. Most people diagnosed with DiGeorge syndrome have a normal life expectancy with mild symptoms [5]. Consistent follow-up is necessary to monitor for potential complications, such as autoimmune diseases and hematologic malignancies [4].

### Epidemiology

DiGeorge syndrome is estimated to occur in approximately 1 in 4,000 live births, making it one of the most common microdeletion syndromes [1]. It affects individuals of all ethnic backgrounds equally.

### History / Nomenclature

DiGeorge syndrome was first described by Dr. Angelo DiGeorge in 1965 [1]. It is also known as 22q11.2 deletion syndrome [1]. The term "CATCH 22" (Cardiac defects, Abnormal facies, Thymic hypoplasia, Cleft palate, Hypocalcemia) was previously used as an acronym to describe the common features of the syndrome. However, this term is now considered outdated because it does not encompass the full spectrum of clinical manifestations and can be stigmatizing [1].

### Research & Trials

Ongoing research aims to better understand the genetic mechanisms underlying DiGeorge syndrome and to develop new treatments and therapies [1]. Studies are focused on identifying modifier genes that influence the severity of the phenotype and on developing targeted therapies to address specific complications, such as immune deficiency and neurodevelopmental disorders.

### Uncertainties & Controversies

While the 22q11.2 deletion is well-established as the cause of DiGeorge syndrome, the precise mechanisms by which the loss of specific genes within the deleted region leads to the diverse range of clinical features remain an area of ongoing research. There is also variability in the expressivity of the syndrome, meaning that even individuals with the same deletion can have very different symptoms and severity. This makes it difficult to predict the long-term prognosis for any given individual.
