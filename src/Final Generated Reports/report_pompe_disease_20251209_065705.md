## Pompe Disease

Pompe disease, also known as glycogen storage disease type II, is a rare and inherited disorder [1, 4]. The condition results from a deficiency in the enzyme acid alpha-glucosidase (GAA) [5]. This enzymatic defect leads to the accumulation of glycogen, a complex sugar, within the body's cells, primarily affecting the heart, skeletal muscles, and respiratory muscles [2, 5]. The buildup of glycogen disrupts the normal function of these vital organs and tissues [1].

Pompe disease presents as a spectrum of conditions, characterized by varying rates of progression and diverse ages of onset [3]. The most severely affected infants typically manifest symptoms within the first three months of life [3]. Treatment options, such as enzyme replacement therapy, are available and can help manage the disease and improve the quality of life for affected individuals [6].

### Overview / Definition

Pompe disease, or glycogen storage disease type II, is a genetic disorder caused by a deficiency in the enzyme acid alpha-glucosidase (GAA) [4, 7]. This enzyme is essential for breaking down glycogen, a stored form of glucose, within lysosomes, which are cellular compartments responsible for waste disposal [2, 5]. When GAA is deficient, glycogen accumulates in various tissues and organs, particularly the heart, muscles, and liver, impairing their function [1]. The disease is classified into different forms based on the age of onset and severity of symptoms, including classic infantile-onset, non-classic infantile-onset, and late-onset Pompe disease [1].

### Signs & Symptoms

The symptoms of Pompe disease vary depending on the type and age of onset [1]. Classic infantile-onset Pompe disease typically manifests within the first few months of life. Infants with this form experience muscle weakness (myopathy), poor muscle tone (hypotonia), an enlarged liver (hepatomegaly), and heart defects [1]. They may also exhibit failure to thrive and have breathing difficulties [1]. Without treatment, classic infantile-onset Pompe disease leads to death from heart failure, usually within the first year of life [1].

Non-classic infantile-onset Pompe disease usually appears by age 1 and is characterized by delayed motor skills and progressive muscle weakness [1]. Although the heart may be abnormally large (cardiomegaly), heart failure is less common [1]. Muscle weakness can lead to serious breathing problems, and most children with this form live only into early childhood [1]. Late-onset Pompe disease (LOPD) emerges in childhood or adulthood and progresses more slowly [4]. Symptoms include proximal muscle weakness and respiratory insufficiency due to diaphragm involvement, while cardiac issues are rare [4]. Over time, muscle weakness worsens [5]. In adults, this can manifest as weakness in the legs, trunk, and arms; shortness of breath; difficulty exercising; and trouble breathing while sleeping [5].

### Genetics & Pathophysiology

Pompe disease is caused by mutations in the GAA gene, which provides instructions for producing the acid alpha-glucosidase enzyme [3]. The disease is inherited in an autosomal recessive pattern [4]. This means that an affected individual must inherit two copies of the mutated gene, one from each parent [5]. Individuals who carry only one copy of the mutated gene are typically asymptomatic carriers [5]. Mutations in the GAA gene lead to a deficiency or complete absence of functional GAA enzyme, resulting in the accumulation of glycogen within lysosomes [1, 4]. This accumulation disrupts normal cellular function and leads to the characteristic symptoms of Pompe disease [1, 2].

### Diagnosis

Diagnosing Pompe disease involves a comprehensive evaluation that includes a physical exam, a review of symptoms, and an assessment of family history [5]. A blood test can measure the activity of the alpha-glucosidase enzyme in the blood [5]. Individuals with Pompe disease will typically have lower than normal or no detectable enzyme activity [5]. Genetic testing can also be performed on a blood sample to identify mutations in the GAA gene [5]. In some cases, a muscle biopsy may be necessary to confirm the diagnosis and assess the extent of glycogen accumulation in muscle tissue [5].

### Management / Treatment

The primary treatment for Pompe disease is enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) [6]. ERT involves intravenous infusions of a genetically engineered enzyme that acts like the naturally occurring GAA enzyme [6]. This helps to break down the accumulated glycogen, improving muscle function, tone, and strength, or at least slowing the progression of the disorder [6]. In addition to ERT, supportive care is essential for managing the symptoms and complications of Pompe disease [6]. This includes physical therapy to maintain muscle strength and mobility, occupational therapy to assist with daily living activities, and respiratory therapy to address breathing problems [6].

### Prognosis / Natural History

The prognosis for individuals with Pompe disease varies depending on the type and age of onset [1, 3]. Classic infantile-onset Pompe disease is the most severe form, and without treatment, it is typically fatal within the first year of life due to heart failure [1]. Non-classic infantile-onset Pompe disease has a slightly better prognosis, but affected children usually only live into early childhood [1]. Late-onset Pompe disease progresses more slowly, and individuals with this form may live into adulthood [4]. However, the disease can still cause significant disability and reduced quality of life [5]. Early diagnosis and treatment with ERT can significantly improve the prognosis for all forms of Pompe disease [6].

### Epidemiology

Pompe disease is a rare disorder, with an estimated incidence of 1 in 40,000 births [1]. The prevalence of the disease may vary among different populations [1].

### History / Nomenclature

Pompe disease was first described in 1932 by Dutch pathologist Johannes Cassianus Pompe, who identified the accumulation of glycogen in the tissues of an infant who died of heart failure [1]. The disease is also known as glycogen storage disease type II or acid maltase deficiency [4].

### Research & Trials

Ongoing research efforts are focused on improving treatment options for Pompe disease and developing new therapies [6]. This includes research into gene therapy, which aims to correct the underlying genetic defect that causes the disease [6]. Clinical trials are also being conducted to evaluate the safety and efficacy of new ERT formulations and other potential treatments [6]. These research efforts offer hope for a better future for individuals with Pompe disease [6].

### Uncertainties & Controversies

While enzyme replacement therapy has significantly improved the outlook for many individuals with Pompe disease, there are still some uncertainties and controversies surrounding its use. The long-term efficacy of ERT is still being studied, and some patients may not respond optimally to treatment [6]. There is also debate about the optimal dosage and timing of ERT, as well as the potential for immune responses to the enzyme [6]. Further research is needed to address these uncertainties and optimize the use of ERT in Pompe disease [6].
