## Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS)

### Lead / Summary

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), also known as Lou Gehrig's disease, is a progressive neurodegenerative disease that affects motor neurons in the brain and spinal cord [1]. This devastating condition leads to a gradual loss of voluntary muscle control, resulting in weakness, muscle atrophy, and eventual paralysis [1]. ALS impacts motor neurons, the nerve cells responsible for controlling voluntary movements such as speaking, chewing, limb movement, and breathing [1].

There is currently no cure for ALS, but treatments are available to help manage symptoms and slow the progression of the disease [1]. These treatments may include medications, physical therapy, and other supportive care measures. Research efforts are ongoing to better understand the causes of ALS and to develop new and more effective therapies [1].

### Overview / Definition

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a neurodegenerative disease characterized by the progressive loss of motor neurons [1]. Motor neurons are nerve cells in the brain and spinal cord that control voluntary muscle movement. As these neurons degenerate, the brain loses its ability to initiate and control muscle movement, leading to muscle weakness, atrophy (muscle wasting), and eventually, paralysis [1].

### Signs & Symptoms

The symptoms of ALS progressively worsen over time [1]. Early symptoms may include muscle weakness or stiffness in the arms or legs, and difficulties with speech or swallowing [1]. As the disease progresses, muscle weakness and atrophy spread to other parts of the body [1]. People with ALS may experience problems with chewing and swallowing (dysphagia), speaking or forming words (dysarthria), and unintended emotional displays [1].

### Genetics & Pathophysiology

The exact cause of ALS is not fully understood, but it is believed to involve a combination of genetic and environmental factors [1]. There are two main types of ALS: sporadic and familial. Sporadic ALS is the most common form, accounting for approximately 90% of cases, and occurs randomly without a known genetic cause or family history [1]. Familial ALS, an inherited form, accounts for the remaining 10% of cases and is caused by inherited gene changes passed down from a parent [1].

In ALS, the degeneration of motor neurons affects the brain and spinal cord, disrupting the signals that control muscle movement [1]. This leads to a progressive loss of voluntary muscle control, resulting in weakness, atrophy, and paralysis.

### Diagnosis

Diagnosing ALS can be challenging, as there is no single test to confirm the diagnosis [1]. Diagnosis is typically based on a clinical examination and a series of diagnostic tests to rule out other conditions that may mimic ALS [1]. These tests may include electrodiagnostic tests such as electromyography (EMG) and nerve conduction velocity (NCV), blood and urine studies, and X-rays, including magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) [1].

### Management / Treatment

Currently, there is no cure for ALS, but treatments are available to help manage symptoms and slow the progression of the disease [1]. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has approved several drugs for ALS that may prolong survival, reduce the rate of decline, or help manage symptoms [1]. These medications include riluzole (Rilutek®, Exservan® and Tiglutik®), edaravone (Radicava® and Radicava ORS®) [1].

### Prognosis / Natural History

The average life expectancy after an ALS diagnosis is two to five years, but some individuals may live longer [1].

### Epidemiology

Sporadic ALS is the most common form of the disease, accounting for approximately 90% of cases [1]. Familial ALS, an inherited form, accounts for the remaining 10% of cases [1].

### History / Nomenclature

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is also known as Lou Gehrig's disease, named after the famous baseball player who was diagnosed with the condition in 1939 [1].

### Research & Trials

Research efforts are ongoing to better understand the causes of ALS and to develop new and more effective therapies [1]. Clinical trials are underway to evaluate potential new treatments for ALS [1].

### Uncertainties & Controversies

The exact cause of sporadic ALS remains unknown, and the relative contributions of genetic and environmental factors are still being investigated [1]. There is ongoing debate about the most effective treatment strategies for ALS, and the optimal timing of interventions [1].
