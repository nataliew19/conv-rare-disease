### Tuberous Sclerosis Complex

Tuberous sclerosis complex (TSC) is a rare genetic disorder that causes non-cancerous tumors to develop in various organs of the body [1, 2]. These tumors can affect the brain, skin, kidneys, heart, lungs, and eyes [1]. The severity of TSC varies significantly from person to person [3]. Some individuals experience few or mild symptoms, while others require continuous medical support [2]. TSC is caused by variants in either the *TSC1* or *TSC2* gene [1].

Although TSC is a genetic condition present from birth, the associated problems may not become apparent until later in life [2]. Many individuals with TSC can lead independent and healthy lives [2]. This article aims to provide a comprehensive overview of TSC, including its causes, symptoms, diagnosis, management, and prognosis.

### Overview / Definition

TSC is a genetic disease that a person is born with [2]. While the genetic mutation is present from birth, the signs and symptoms of TSC might not be immediately obvious [2]. TSC is caused by variants in the *TSC1* or *TSC2* gene [1]. These genes provide instructions for making proteins called hamartin and tuberin, which help regulate cell growth and division [1]. When these genes are not functioning correctly, it can lead to the development of tumors in different organs.

### Signs & Symptoms

Symptoms of TSC can vary widely, but some common manifestations include seizures, kidney problems, developmental delay, skin issues, and lung involvement [1, 3]. Seizures, including infantile spasms, focal seizures, and tonic-clonic seizures, are often seen in children with TSC [1]. Kidney issues can include cysts and angiomyolipomas, which are benign growths made of fatty tissue and muscle cells [1]. Developmental delay and TSC-associated neuropsychiatric disorders (TAND) are also common [1]. TAND encompasses a range of cognitive, behavioral, and psychiatric conditions such as aggression, anxiety, attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, and obsessive-compulsive disorder [1]. Skin issues can include white or lighter patches of skin (called ash leaf spots), raised red or darker spots or bumps on the face (called angiofibromas), areas of thick, pebbled skin on the lower back or neck (called shagreen patches), and small growths under the nails (called ungual or subungual fibromas) [1]. Lung involvement, particularly lymphangioleiomyomatosis (LAM), a rare lung disorder primarily affecting women of childbearing age or older, can also occur [1].

### Genetics & Pathophysiology

TSC is caused by mutations in either the *TSC1* or *TSC2* gene [1]. These genes are responsible for producing proteins called hamartin and tuberin, respectively, which act as tumor suppressors by regulating cell growth and proliferation [1]. Mutations in these genes disrupt this regulation, leading to uncontrolled cell growth and the formation of tumors in various organs [1].

### Diagnosis

Diagnosis of TSC typically involves a combination of clinical evaluation, imaging studies, and genetic testing [1]. A physical examination may reveal characteristic skin findings such as ash leaf spots or angiofibromas [1]. Imaging tests, such as MRI of the brain and kidneys, can help identify tumors or other abnormalities [1]. Genetic testing can confirm the diagnosis by detecting mutations in the *TSC1* or *TSC2* gene [1].

### Diagnostic Criteria

Diagnostic criteria for TSC include major and minor features. Major features include facial angiofibromas or cephalic plaque, ungual fibromas, hypomelanotic macules (ash leaf spots), shagreen patch, cortical tubers, subependymal nodules, subependymal giant cell astrocytoma (SEGA), cardiac rhabdomyoma, lymphangioleiomyomatosis (LAM), and angiomyolipoma [1]. Minor features include multiple confetti skin lesions, dental enamel pits, intraoral fibromas, retinal achromic patch, multiple renal cysts, and nonrenal hamartoma [1]. A definite diagnosis of TSC requires either two major features or one major feature with two minor features.

### Testing

Genetic testing is available to identify mutations in the *TSC1* and *TSC2* genes [1]. This testing can be helpful in confirming a diagnosis of TSC, especially in individuals with subtle or atypical presentations [1].

### Management / Treatment

There is currently no cure for TSC [4]. Treatment focuses on managing the symptoms and preventing complications [4, 5]. This may involve medications to control seizures, therapies to address developmental and behavioral issues, and surgical interventions to remove or reduce the size of tumors [1, 4, 5]. Targeted therapies, such as mTOR inhibitors (e.g., everolimus), can be used to manage certain tumor growths and improve outcomes [5].

### Managing Seizures

Seizures are a common symptom of TSC and are typically managed with anti-seizure medications [1]. The choice of medication depends on the type and frequency of seizures [1]. In some cases, surgery may be an option for controlling seizures that are not responsive to medication [1].

### Managing Skin Problems

Skin manifestations of TSC, such as angiofibromas, can be treated with laser therapy or other dermatological procedures [1]. These treatments can help improve the appearance of the skin and reduce discomfort [1].

### Managing Kidney Problems

Kidney angiomyolipomas are monitored regularly to assess their size and growth rate [1]. If they become large or cause symptoms, interventions such as embolization or surgery may be necessary [1].

### Managing Lung Problems (LAM)

Lymphangioleiomyomatosis (LAM) is treated with mTOR inhibitors, which can help slow the progression of the disease [1]. Other treatments may include bronchodilators and oxygen therapy [1].

### Managing Behavioral and Learning Challenges

Therapies and interventions for TAND (TSC-associated neuropsychiatric disorders) include behavioral therapy, educational support, and medication [1]. These interventions aim to address cognitive, behavioral, and psychiatric challenges associated with TSC [1].

### Surveillance and Monitoring

Regular check-ups and monitoring are essential for individuals with TSC to detect and manage potential complications [5]. These check-ups may include imaging studies, blood tests, and developmental assessments [5].

### Prognosis / Natural History

The prognosis for individuals with TSC varies depending on the severity of the disease and the specific organs affected [3]. Early identification and intervention can help improve outcomes and quality of life [3]. Some individuals with TSC may experience mild symptoms and live relatively normal lives, while others may face significant challenges and require ongoing medical care [3].

### Epidemiology

TSC affects approximately 1 million people worldwide [2]. It occurs in all races and ethnic groups [2]. The estimated incidence of TSC is 1 in 6,000 live births [2].

### History / Nomenclature

TSC was first described by Désiré-Magloire Bourneville in 1880, who called it "sclérose tubéreuse des circonvolutions cérébrales" (tuberous sclerosis of the cerebral convolutions). The term "tuberous sclerosis" refers to the characteristic tuber-like lesions found in the brain of affected individuals.

### Research & Trials

Ongoing research efforts are focused on developing new treatments and finding a cure for TSC [5]. These efforts include studies to better understand the genetic and molecular mechanisms underlying TSC, as well as clinical trials to evaluate new therapies [5].

### Uncertainties & Controversies

While significant progress has been made in understanding and managing TSC, several uncertainties and controversies remain. One area of debate is the optimal timing and type of interventions for specific manifestations of TSC. For example, the decision to treat small, asymptomatic kidney angiomyolipomas with mTOR inhibitors or to monitor them closely is often individualized based on patient-specific factors. Another area of uncertainty is the long-term effects of mTOR inhibitors on growth and development, particularly in children. Further research is needed to address these uncertainties and optimize the management of TSC.
